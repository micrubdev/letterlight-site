Seneca greets his Lucilius.

You tell me you are exhausted, and not in the honest way a farmer is tired after the harvest. You sleep and wake up tired. You take a few days by the sea and come back as heavy as you left. You say that the work you once chose gladly now feels like a debt you can never finish paying, and that you have started to dread the sound of messages arriving.

I believe you, and I am not going to tell you to be grateful for your position. I have held high positions. I know how a full calendar can look like success from the outside and feel like a prison from the inside.

Let me tell you what I have noticed about people in your state. They are almost never lazy. They are usually the ones who said yes too many times. Each yes was reasonable on its own. Together they built a house with no door. And because each duty arrived politely, one at a time, no single one of them seems worth refusing.

So the first thing I ask is that you take an honest account of your days, the way a careful steward checks the accounts of an estate. Where do your hours go? How many belong to work that matters, how many to work that only looks like it matters, and how many to worrying about work while pretending to rest? Most people who do this are shocked. They find that a large share of their life was spent on things they would never have chosen if anyone had asked them plainly.

The second thing is harder. You have tried to cure this by changing your surroundings: a holiday, a new desk, a different city for a weekend. These are pleasant, and they do almost nothing. You take yourself with you on every journey. If you carry the same anxious habits and the same inability to say no, you will be just as tired by a lake as you are in your office. The change has to happen inside first.

What does that look like in practice? Start small, because a tired man who makes a grand plan only adds one more burden.

Choose one obligation this week and give it back. Not the most important one, and not the easiest one either. Pick the one you resent most and that would cost the least to drop. Write the message, send it, and notice that the world goes on.

Protect one hour each day that belongs to no one. No meetings, no messages, no productive hobby that secretly becomes another task. Read something old. Walk without counting your steps. Sit in the garden and let your mind wander where it likes. At first you will feel guilty, as if you were stealing. You are not stealing. You are taking back what was yours.

Sleep like it is part of your work, because it is. I have seen clever men ruin their judgment by going without sleep and then wonder why every problem looked enormous.

And think about what you are working for. Many people in your situation are not exhausted by the work itself. They are exhausted by working hard for things they stopped believing in long ago. If you are building a fortune you have no time to enjoy, or chasing a title that will mean nothing to you once you hold it, then rest alone will not cure you. You will need to ask what a good life would look like and then begin, slowly, to move your days toward it.

I do not say leave your post tomorrow. Retirement on a whim is as foolish as staying forever out of fear. But a person can step back in stages. You can do less of what drains you and more of what you came to this work to do. You can let others carry their share. You can stop answering at midnight.

Write to me in a fortnight and tell me which duty you gave back, and how you spent your hour. I will want details.
