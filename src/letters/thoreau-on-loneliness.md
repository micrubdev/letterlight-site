Dear neighbour,

You write that you moved to a new city in the spring, that you work from a small apartment, and that whole days go by when you speak to no one but the woman at the bakery. You write that the evenings are the worst. You ask how I could have chosen to live alone in a hut by a pond for two years, and whether I was not lonely.

I was, sometimes. I will not pretend otherwise. There was an afternoon in my first weeks at Walden, in a gentle rain, when I felt suddenly that I could not be well without company nearby. It lasted about an hour. Then, while the rain kept falling, I began to notice the drops pattering on the roof, the pines, the sound of the water, and I felt something I can only call friendliness, as if the whole world around my house had become company. The loneliness did not come back in the same way after that.

I tell you this because I think there are two different things that both get called loneliness. One is missing people: a real hunger, and it needs real food, which is people. I will come to that. The other is being uneasy in your own company. That second kind is often the louder one, and it is the one most people try to cure with noise.

Look at how you spend those evenings. Is the television on? The phone in your hand? I do not scold you. I only ask whether the noise is filling the silence or just keeping you from hearing it. A great many people, I have noticed, are not so much lonely as they are afraid of being alone with themselves. They fill every minute so that they never have to find out who is there.

Try finding out. Not in a grand way. Take a long walk with no destination, and leave the phone behind. Go at the hour you dread, just after work, when the light is changing. Walk until you stop thinking about walking. Look at what is around you: which trees are in the park, which birds are at the feeder in the courtyard, the way the brick of the old buildings holds the evening sun. You will find more going on than you suspected. The world is very busy, and very little of it is lonely.

Then sit somewhere and write. Not to anyone in particular. Just what you saw, and what you thought about it. You may find that you are better company than you believed. I found that I could spend a whole morning sitting in my doorway, watching the light move across the pond, and come away feeling as if I had been in the best conversation of my life.

Now the first kind, the hunger for people. That is real, and solitude does not cure it. I had friends. I walked into Concord most days. I had visitors at the hut, and I kept three chairs: one for solitude, two for friendship, three for society. You need all three.

So make the effort, small as it is. Say a little more to the woman at the bakery each time; ask her name. Join something that meets every week, a choir, a walking group, a class in something you have always been curious about, so that you see the same faces until they become familiar. Write a real letter, on paper, to a friend you have drifted from. These things feel awkward at first. Do them anyway.

You will notice that when you become easier in your own company, you also become easier company for others. People feel it. You no longer need them quite so desperately, and so they find it simpler to be near you.

Simplify. Walk. Watch. Write to someone. The pond is always there, in some form, wherever you live.
