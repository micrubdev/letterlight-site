To a young friend who measures himself against others,

Your letter reached me, and I read it twice. You write that wherever you look you see people ahead of you. A classmate who earns more, a cousin whose children are better behaved, a colleague whose name is spoken in rooms you never enter. You feel small beside them. Some days you wish them misfortune, and then feel ashamed of the wish.

I thank you for your honesty. Many people feel this and pretend they do not. A person who can admit a fault has already begun to correct it.

Let me tell you something of my own life. For many years I hoped to serve a ruler who would govern well and let me help him. I travelled from state to state with my students, and again and again I was turned away, while other men, less learned and less careful, were given office. I will not pretend this never stung. But in time I came to see that the post was not the point. The point was to become the kind of person who would be worthy of it, whether or not it was ever offered.

You are looking at others the way a merchant looks at a rival's shop, counting what they have and you lack. There is a better way to look. When you see someone of real quality, someone kind, skilled, steady, honest, let the sight become a lesson instead of a wound. Ask yourself: what does this person do each day that I do not? How do they speak to their parents? How do they prepare their work? How do they behave when no one is watching? These things can be learned. They are not handed out by fortune.

And when you see someone behaving badly, someone arrogant or careless or cruel, do not stop at judging them. Look for the same seed in yourself. It is there more often than we like to admit.

This way, every person you meet becomes your teacher. Those you admire show you where to go. Those you do not admire show you what to avoid. Envy cannot grow in a mind that is busy learning.

Notice, too, that what you envy is mostly the outside of things. Salary, rank, reputation. These are like the fine robes worn at a ceremony. They tell you little about the person inside. I have known men in fine robes who were hollow, and men in rough cloth who were the best of their generation. Admire the person, not the robe.

Now, the practical part, because good intentions without practice are like a field ploughed and never sown.

Choose one quality you admire in someone else, only one. Perhaps patience. Perhaps diligence. Perhaps the way they listen. Practise it in the smallest daily acts: how you greet your family in the morning, how carefully you finish one task, how you answer a tiresome question. Do this for a season. You will be surprised how much changes.

Spend less time where comparison is sharpest. If certain gatherings or certain kinds of talk always leave you feeling small, attend them less. A wise person chooses their company and their surroundings with care.

My student Zengzi examined himself every day on three points: whether he had been loyal in working for others, whether he had kept faith with his friends, and whether he had practised what he was taught. You could do worse than borrow his questions each evening. Do not ask whether you were ahead of anyone. That is not a question worth losing sleep over.

And be patient. Self-cultivation is slow, like a tree growing. You will not see it from day to day. But one year you will look up and find that the person you once envied is someone you can now meet as an equal, or even help.

The measured path is long, but it is yours, and no one else can walk it for you.
