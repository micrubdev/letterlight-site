My friend,

You write that your temper is short and that you are tired of the damage it does. A colleague contradicts you in a meeting and your face burns. Your teenager rolls their eyes and you hear yourself shouting. Afterwards you are sorry, but the words have already been said. You ask whether patience can be learned, or whether some people are simply born with it.

Let me tell you a story they used to tell about me.

Once a man made a bet with a friend that he could make me lose my temper. Four hundred zuz was at stake, which is a great deal of money. It was the eve of the Sabbath, and I was dressing for it. He came to my door shouting, "Where is Hillel? Where is Hillel?" without any courtesy at all. I put on my cloak and went out to him and asked what he wanted.

"Why do the Babylonians have round heads?" he asked.

It was a foolish question, meant to insult me, since I came from Babylon myself. I answered it as well as I could. He went away and came back an hour later, shouting again, with another foolish question. And again an hour after that. Each time I went out, and each time I answered. At last he said he had many more questions but was afraid I would get angry. I told him to ask whatever he liked. Then he tried insulting me to my face, and finally told me that because of me he had lost four hundred zuz. I said that it was better for him to lose his money than for Hillel to lose his patience.

I tell you this not to boast. I tell it because it shows something about anger. The man thought he was testing me. In truth he was testing himself, and he was the one who walked away upset.

People believe patience is a gift some are born with. I believe it is more like a muscle. Every time you answer a provocation gently, it grows a little stronger. Every time you let it go and shout, it grows weaker. You have years of practice in one direction. You can begin practising in the other today.

Here is how I would begin.

First, slow down the moment before you answer. The angry reply comes fast. The wise reply comes second. Put something between the insult and your response: a breath, a sip of water, a silent count. In my day we said a short blessing before eating, which made us pause before we grabbed. You can make your own pause.

Second, remember that what is hateful to you, you should not do to your neighbour. This is the heart of everything I taught. When you are about to snap, ask yourself: would I want to be spoken to like this? If you would not, then the words are not yours to say, however much they were provoked.

Third, judge others kindly, as you would want to be judged. You do not know what your colleague endured this morning, or what your child is afraid of. Assume, at least at first, that there is a reason you cannot see. Very often there is.

Fourth, when you fail, and you will, repair it quickly. Go back and say you were wrong to speak so harshly. A short-tempered person cannot teach, but a person who apologises teaches more than they know. Your children will learn more from watching you make amends than from never seeing you fall.

If your anger has turned into violence, or frightens the people who live with you, seek help from someone trained to give it. That is no disgrace. It is the beginning of teshuvah, returning to the person you want to be.

For the next week, choose one person who often tries your patience. Each time they do, answer them a little more slowly and a little more softly than you want to. Keep a note each evening of how it went. You have waited long enough to start, and if not now, when?
