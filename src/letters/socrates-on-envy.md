My friend,

You write that you cannot stop measuring yourself against other people. A school friend has a bigger house. A colleague got the promotion. Strangers on your phone seem to travel, marry and succeed while you stand still. You feel small, and then you feel ashamed of feeling small.

I am not the right man to give you a cure, since I have none. But I can ask you questions, and perhaps between us we can find out what this envy is made of.

First: when you look at your colleague's promotion, what exactly do you want? Is it the title? The money? Or is it what you imagine they feel when people look at them? These are different things. A man who wants the money might be satisfied by a better-paid job somewhere else. A man who wants admiration may never be satisfied, because admiration is held in other people's hands, and they can always take it back.

Second question. How much do you know about the life you envy? You see your friend's house. Do you see the debt, the quarrels in the kitchen, the nights he lies awake? You compare your inside, which you know completely, with his outside, which he has arranged for you to see. Is that a fair contest? If you set your worst day against his best photograph, who would win?

I spent my life in the marketplace of Athens, talking with people who were thought to be wise: politicians, poets, craftsmen. I went to them hoping to find someone wiser than I was. And I found something strange. Many of them knew less than they believed. The reputation was larger than the thing. I came away thinking that at least I knew how little I knew, and they did not even know that. Since then I have wondered how many of the lives we envy are like those reputations. Bright on the outside. Ordinary within.

Third question, and this is the one I asked my fellow citizens until they grew tired of me. What are you caring for? Most people spend their lives gathering money and honour and the good opinion of others, and give hardly a thought to the condition of their own soul: whether they are just, whether they are honest, whether they understand anything. Envy is a sign that you are measuring yourself on someone else's scale. You are asking, who has more? But more of what? If you are measuring houses, someone will always have a bigger one. If you are measuring whether you were kind today, or learned something, or kept your word, then the only person to compare with is the one you were yesterday.

I was poor, and I walked barefoot through the city, and I was not a handsome man. If I had compared myself to the rich young men who followed me around, I would have been miserable. Instead I found that I had something they wanted, which was the habit of asking what things are worth. You may have something like that too, unnoticed because you are always looking at someone else's hand.

Here is something else to consider. Is envy useful to you at all? Sometimes it is a teacher. If you envy a friend who writes, perhaps you want to write. Then the envy has shown you a desire, and you can thank it and go and write. But if you envy someone for something you do not actually want, or cannot have, then it is only pain with no lesson in it, and you may let it go.

So let us make a small experiment, since I trust experiments more than advice. For one week, each time you catch yourself comparing, stop and ask three things. What do I want here? Do I actually know their life? And by whose scale am I measuring? Write down the answers. At the end of the week, read them back. I suspect you will find the same few wishes underneath, and they will be clearer than the envy was.

And if the comparing is fed by something you look at every day, a screen full of other people's best moments, you might ask whether you need to look so often. A man who stands in front of a bakery all day will feel hungry.

You do not need to win against anyone. You need to know what is worth winning.
