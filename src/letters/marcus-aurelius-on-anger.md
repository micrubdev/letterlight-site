To a friend who is angry,

You write that people are making you furious. A manager who takes credit for your work, a neighbour who plays music at midnight, a relative who says the same cruel thing at every gathering. You lie awake composing replies. You ask how I, who had to deal with senators, generals and courtiers all my life, kept from losing my temper.

The truth is that I did not always keep from it. My notebooks are full of reminders to myself on this very subject, which tells you how often I needed them. A man does not write the same advice to himself twenty times unless he keeps forgetting it.

When I was young, I had teachers who showed me another way. One of them, Rusticus, made peace easily with people who had wronged him. Another, Sextus, kept free of anger and yet was one of the most affectionate men I knew. I watched them and wanted to be like them. I am still trying.

Here is what I learned from them, and from many years of difficult people.

First, stop being surprised. Friction between people is as ordinary as weather. A sailor does not rage at the wind for blowing. He adjusts his sails. When you treat a rude remark as weather rather than as an outrage, half its power is gone.

Second, remember that they are doing what seems right to them. Very few people wake up intending to be wicked. The manager who takes your credit is afraid for his position. The neighbour is careless, not cruel. The relative is unhappy in ways you may not see. This does not make what they do acceptable. But it changes your anger into something closer to pity, and pity is easier to carry.

Third, look at the cost. Ask yourself honestly: what has this anger done to me? It has spoiled my evening. It has kept me awake. It has made me short with people who did nothing wrong. Often the offence itself took a minute, and the anger has lasted a week. The other person has long since forgotten it. You are still paying.

Fourth, ask whether you have ever done the same thing. I have been careless. I have been unkind. I have taken credit that was not wholly mine. When I remember this, it becomes harder to stand in judgment.

Fifth, remember how short life is. In a few years, you and the person who angers you will both be gone, and the quarrel with you. This is not meant to be gloomy. It is meant to put the matter at its proper size. Is this worth one of your limited days?

Now, a practical habit. When you feel anger rising, pause and name it to yourself: "This is anger. It is a judgment I am making, not a fact about the world." Then ask: what is actually in front of me? A rude email is a few lines of text. A late colleague is a person who arrived after the hour. Strip away the story and look at the plain fact. It is usually much smaller.

And when you must respond, respond with justice, not with anger. You may still need to speak plainly to the manager, or ask the neighbour to lower the music. Do it calmly and firmly, as a doctor treats a patient: without hatred, only with the aim of setting things right. Gentleness is not weakness. In my experience, it takes more strength to stay gentle than to shout.

If your anger is hurting the people you love, or you find yourself unable to stop once it starts, seek help from a physician or counsellor. That is not a failure. It is wisdom.

Try the pause once tomorrow. Then write down what happened, and how long the anger lasted.
