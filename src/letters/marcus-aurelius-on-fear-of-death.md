To a friend who has started to count the years,

You write that you have reached the age when death is no longer an idea. Your parents are old, friends have fallen ill, and you have begun to notice your own body changing. At night you think of the end and feel a cold, helpless fear. You ask how I, who wrote so often about death, managed to live with it.

I will not pretend I was never afraid. I wrote about death so often precisely because I needed to keep looking at it until it stopped frightening me.

I had more reason than most to think about it. During my reign a great plague swept through the empire. It reached the army camps and the cities alike, and it took soldiers, citizens and friends without asking their rank. My brother emperor, Lucius, died while travelling with me. And my wife and I buried many of our children, some of them very small. I do not tell you this so that you will pity me. I tell you so that you know I am not writing from comfort.

Here is what helped.

First, I reminded myself that death is natural. It belongs to the same order as birth, growth and the turning of the seasons. Everything that is put together will one day come apart, and its parts will be used again by nature for something new. When I looked at death this way, as a process rather than a punishment, it became less like an enemy waiting in the dark and more like evening at the end of a day.

Second, I noticed that most of my fear was about time I did not yet have. I worried about a future death while sitting in a living present, with work to do and people to care for. The fear was always about some later hour, and meanwhile this hour slipped by unused. So I tried to bring my attention back, again and again, to the thing in front of me.

Third, I asked myself a plain question: am I doing what I should be doing, right now? Fear of death often hides a different fear, which is the fear of having lived badly. If I was being just, honest and kind in the task in front of me, there was little left for the fear to hold on to. If I was being lazy, cruel or false, then the fear had a point, and the answer was to change, not to worry.

Fourth, I tried to stop clinging. We hold our lives as if they were a possession we had bought and might be robbed of, and so we spend them guarding instead of living. I do not mean this to sound cold. I loved my children and my friends fiercely. But I tried to love them as one loves the spring: with full attention, knowing it will pass, and grateful that it came at all.

Now, something practical.

When the fear wakes you at night, do not argue with it in the dark. Get up, light a lamp, and write down what you are afraid of, as precisely as you can. Fears that are written down are smaller than fears that circle in the head.

Each morning, before the day begins, take one breath and remember that this day is not guaranteed. Then ask what one good thing you will do with it. Not a great thing. A kind word, honest work, a call to someone you love.

Spend time with old people, and listen to them. Many of them have made their peace with the end in ways that books cannot teach.

And look after your health sensibly, then leave the rest. Watching your body for every sign of illness will not lengthen your life by one day. It will only fill the days you have with worry.

If your fear grows so strong that it keeps you from sleeping, working or enjoying anything for weeks, speak to a physician. There is no shame in that. Even an emperor needs a good doctor.

Tonight, write the fear down. Tomorrow, do one good thing. That is enough to begin.
