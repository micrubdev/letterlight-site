Dear friend,

You write that the thought of death has started to follow you around. It comes when you are brushing your teeth, or sitting in traffic, or watching your children sleep. You ask what the point of anything is if it all ends, and you say you are embarrassed to ask, because everyone else seems to carry on as if the question did not exist.

Do not be embarrassed. I asked the same question for most of my life, and I asked it badly for a long time before I asked it well.

When I was young I watched my brother Nikolai die of consumption. He was the best of us, clever and gentle, and he died slowly in a rented room in the south of France. I sat beside him and could do nothing. Afterwards I felt that if death could take a man like him so easily, then everything we did was a kind of play-acting. I went back to my estate and my writing, but the feeling did not leave.

Years later, travelling to look at some land I meant to buy, I stopped for the night at an inn in a small town called Arzamas. I lay down in a clean, whitewashed room and was suddenly seized by a terror I cannot fully describe. It was not fear of anything in particular. It was death itself, standing in the room, asking me what I was doing with my life. I did not sleep. For many years afterwards I remembered that room.

So I know the cold you are feeling. And I want to tell you what I learned, slowly, and against my own stubbornness.

The first thing is this. The fear of death is very often a fear of having lived wrongly. I wrote a story once about a judge named Ivan Ilych, a respectable man who did everything that was expected of him. He had a good position, a proper house, correct friends. When he fell ill and saw that he was dying, what tormented him most was not the pain. It was the growing suspicion that his whole life had been arranged for appearances, and that he had never really loved anyone or been truly useful to them. Only at the very end, when he stopped defending his life and felt pity for the people beside him, did the terror leave him.

I did not invent that story out of nothing. I wrote it because I recognised myself in it.

So when the fear comes, do not only ask, "How can I stop being afraid?" Ask also, "Is there something in how I live that I already know is false?" Often there is. A job kept only for the sake of what others think. A quarrel you have let harden. A kindness you keep postponing. These things weigh on us, and death makes the weight felt.

The second thing I learned is that meaning is not found by thinking alone. I spent years reading philosophers, hoping one of them would solve the riddle for me. They described the problem beautifully and left me exactly where I was. What helped me was watching the peasants on my estate. They worked hard, they suffered, they buried their children, and yet many of them faced death with a calm I did not have. They did not have better arguments. They had a way of living: work with the hands, care for one another, a simple faith that life was given for some purpose larger than themselves.

I am not asking you to become a peasant, or to adopt my beliefs. I am asking you to notice that the question "What is the meaning of life?" is answered less by an idea than by a day. Go out and do some plain, useful work. Dig a garden bed, cook for someone, mend something broken. I took up ploughing and making boots in my old age, and my family thought me ridiculous. But I slept better, and the cold thoughts came less often.

The third thing is love, though I know the word is worn thin. Each of us will die, and we cannot change that. What we can change is whether we spend our days caring only for ourselves or caring for others. When I live for myself alone, death seems to cancel everything. When I live for others, what I give does not vanish with me. It goes on in them.

Here is something practical. Tonight, before you sleep, write down three things you did today. Then ask of each one: would I be content if this were among the last things I did? You will not always answer yes. That is fine. The question is not meant to shame you, only to turn you, little by little, toward the life you actually want.

And if the fear grows so large that it keeps you from working or sleeping for weeks, please talk to a doctor or someone you trust. There is no shame in needing company for this.

You asked what the point is. I cannot give you a formula. But I can tell you that the people I have known who feared death least were not the ones who thought about it least. They were the ones who loved most, and wasted the fewest days pretending.
