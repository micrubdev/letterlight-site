To a friend who feels alone,

You tell me that the evenings are the worst. The day keeps you busy, but when the door closes there is no one to tell how it went, and the quiet starts to feel like a verdict. You wonder whether something in you keeps people away.

I will tell you what I know about this. Parts of my notebook were written in army camps on the Danube, far from Rome, among soldiers who saluted me and could not speak to me as an equal. An emperor is rarely alone and almost never in company. Everyone wanted something from me, and so I could trust very few of them. I know the feeling of sitting by a lamp with a thousand men within shouting distance and no one to talk to.

So I will not tell you that loneliness is nothing. It hurts, and the hurt has a purpose. Hunger tells the body it needs food. Loneliness tells you that you need other people, and that is no defect. Nature made us for one another the way she made two hands for one body. A hand cut off and lying apart from the rest is not free. It is simply useless and sad. When you ache for company, it is your nature reminding you what you are for.

That means the first thing is to stop treating the ache as proof that you are unlovable. It proves you are human. Every person you pass in the street has felt it. Many of them feel it tonight.

The second thing is to separate what is in your power from what is not. Whether a particular person calls you back is not in your power. Whether you call them is. Whether you are invited is not in your power. Whether you show up where people gather, and stay a little longer than is comfortable, is. You cannot command friendship, but you can keep making yourself available to it, the way a farmer cannot command rain but can keep the field ploughed.

In the first book of my notebook I wrote down what I owed to each person who had shaped me. My grandfather, for good temper. My mother, for simple living and a generous hand. My teacher Rusticus, for lending me books and showing me how to write a plain letter. When I felt cut off from everyone, writing that list reminded me that I was made of other people. You are too. Try it. Write down five people who gave you something you still carry. Some may be dead or far away. Some you may be able to thank this week. A letter of thanks is one of the easiest doors back into another person's life.

Third, look at how you spend your solitude. There are two ways of being alone. One is exile, where you sit waiting for someone to rescue you and every silent hour counts against you. The other is retreat. People pay for houses by the sea and in the mountains to get away from others, and yet you can retire into your own mind whenever you choose, if you have furnished it with good thoughts. Read something worth reading. Walk without your phone. Cook properly for one person, as if that person mattered, because they do. Solitude that is spent well makes you better company when company comes.

Fourth, be useful to someone. This is the shortest road I know out of loneliness. Help a neighbour carry something. Volunteer for work that needs hands. Teach what you know. When you act for the common good, you take your place again in the body you belong to, and the sense of being a severed limb begins to fade. You do not need to wait until you feel connected to start acting connected. Feeling often follows action.

I should add that loneliness that lasts for months can weigh on a person's health like any other illness. If yours has settled in deep, if it keeps you from sleeping or from caring about anything, tell a doctor, as a sensible person would with any wound that will not close.

Tonight, then, do one small thing in each direction. Send one message to someone you have lost touch with, without waiting to see if they answer. And spend one hour of the evening alone on purpose, doing something you would choose even if the phone rang.

You were made for others, and others were made for you. Some of them are looking for you as well.
