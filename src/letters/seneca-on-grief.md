Seneca greets his friend.

Your letter came this morning, and I read it twice before I could answer. You write that he died on Thursday, that the funeral is next week, and that you keep picking up your phone to tell him something. You also write, almost in passing, that people have told you to be strong. I want to begin there.

Do not be strong in the way they mean. I have written a great deal about keeping the mind steady, and I stand by most of it, but I have never asked anyone to pretend that a death is nothing. When we lose a friend, the eyes should not be dry. That would not be wisdom. It would be a kind of numbness, and numbness is not peace. Cry when the tears come. Cry in the car, in the shower, in the middle of the supermarket if you must. You are not failing at anything.

What I would ask of you is something smaller and harder: let the grief be grief, and do not let it turn into a storm you add to on purpose. There is a weeping that honours the dead and a wailing that is partly for the audience, partly for ourselves, partly a way of proving how much we loved. You do not have to prove anything. He knew.

I lost a dear friend once, too young and too suddenly, and I am ashamed to say I mourned him badly. I wept more than I should have, and I see now why. I had never thought he could die before me. I had lived as if we had an endless supply of evenings. That was my mistake, and it made the blow heavier. I tell you this not to scold you, but because there is a gentler way to carry what you are carrying.

It is this. Turn, slowly, from what you have lost to what you had. Not today, perhaps. Today may be only for missing him. But in the weeks ahead, try to let your memory of him become a pleasant thing rather than only a wound. Remember his jokes, the way he argued, the dreadful coffee he insisted on making. At first every memory will sting. In time, if you let it, the sting will fade and the sweetness will stay. Fruit that is a little sour at first can be the best of all, and the same is true of remembering a friend.

Some people try to escape grief by keeping busy every waking hour, or by never speaking of the dead. I understand the wish, but it rarely works. The grief waits for them, patiently, and comes back at three in the morning. Better to sit with it in daylight, a little at a time. Speak his name. Tell the stories. Let other people tell theirs. You will learn things about him you never knew, and he will be a little more alive for it.

Look after your body, too, though it will feel absurd. Eat something, even if it tastes of nothing. Go outside once a day. Sleep when sleep comes. Grief is heavy work, and a body running on nothing makes everything harder to bear.

And do not go through this alone. You have friends who loved him and friends who love you. Let them bring food, sit with you, say the wrong thing. If the weight becomes too much, if you find the days going dark in a way that frightens you, speak to someone whose work is to help: a doctor, a counsellor, one of the lines on this page. There is no shame in it. The strongest people I have known were the ones who knew when to ask.

Write to me again after the funeral. Tell me one thing about him that made you laugh. I would like to know him a little, through you.
