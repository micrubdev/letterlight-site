Seneca greets his friend.

You write that they called you into a small room, spoke kindly for four minutes, and by noon your badge no longer opened the door. You write that you walked home in the middle of a working day and the streets looked strange, full of people who seemed to have somewhere to be. And you ask me, half joking, whether a philosopher has anything to offer a man with rent due and no salary.

I have something. Not money, I am sorry to say, though I have had plenty and lost some of it, and I can tell you the losing taught me more than the having. What I can offer is a way of looking at this week so that it does less damage than it wants to.

First, count what has actually happened. You have lost an income and a place to go each morning. That is real and I will not pretend otherwise. But notice how much more your mind has added since. It has already sold your house, shamed you at a dinner you have not been invited to yet, and decided that no one will ever hire you again. None of these things has happened. You are suffering today for a dozen tomorrows, most of which will never arrive in the shape you fear. I have watched myself do the same thing for years, and I wrote it down once so that I would stop. It did not entirely work. It helped.

So divide your troubles into two piles. In one, put what is true this morning: the account balance, the date the last payment arrives, the people who owe you a reference. In the other, put the stories: what your brother will say, what your former colleagues are whispering, the version of you at fifty still looking for work. Deal with the first pile with a pen and a list. The second pile you may look at, but do not let it give orders.

Second, consider what the job was. You were good at it, I know. But you also told me, more than once, that you ate lunch at your desk and answered messages at the dinner table and could not remember the last book you finished. A job is a fine thing to have and a poor thing to be. If you were your work, then today you are nothing, and that is plainly false, because here you are writing me a long and rather witty letter. The man who writes letters is still here. Find out what else he does.

Third, be careful with your time. This is where most people in your position go wrong, and I say it because I have seen it in men far richer than you. The first week feels like a holiday, the second like a punishment, and by the third the days have no edges at all. Keep the edges. Rise at the hour you used to rise. Give the morning to the search, the letters, the calls: honest work, done properly. Give the afternoon to something that is only yours: walking, reading, the friend you have not seen since spring. In the evening, stop. Unemployment is not a reason to work twenty hours a day at worrying.

Fourth, do not hide. Shame tells you to wait until you have good news before you speak to anyone. That is backwards. The people who will help you find the next thing are the ones who know you are looking. Tell them plainly, without apology. Most of them have been where you are or are afraid of it, and they will respect you more for saying it out loud.

Last, a small exercise, the kind I set myself. Once this week, spend a day living cheaply on purpose. Plain food, a coat you already own, no purchase that is not needed. Then ask yourself: is this the condition I feared? You will find that you can stand a great deal less than you thought, and be content in it. A person who knows they can live on little is very hard to frighten.

You will work again, and probably soon. When you do, I hope you take with you what this gap can teach: that you are more than the door your badge opens, and that the worst of most troubles happens in the mind before it happens anywhere else.

Write to me when you have sent the first ten applications. I want to hear which one you were proudest of.
