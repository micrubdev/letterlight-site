My dear idler,

Forgive the greeting. You used the word yourself four times, and I thought I would save you the trouble of a fifth.

Your letter is eleven pages long. It is lively and well constructed, with a digression on the nature of the will that I read aloud to a visitor. It explains why you have not begun your thesis. I counted the pages with some sadness, because if you had written those instead, you would have had a chapter.

I know your type well and have a great weakness for it. I put one of you in a play. Trofimov is a student, not yet thirty, who has been at the university so long that people call him the eternal student. He makes a fine speech in the second act about how educated Russians do nothing but talk, and how everyone must work. It is a good speech. He is quite right. He then goes on not finishing his degree. Is he noble or ridiculous? I can't see why he should have to choose.

You, too, are right about everything. You have diagnosed yourself with perfectionism, with fear of judgment, with a distracted age. All true, I daresay. But I am a doctor, and I have noticed that some patients become so fond of the diagnosis that they forget to take the medicine.

The medicine is dull. Here it is.

Work at the same hour every day, for a short time, whether or not you feel like it. Half an hour is plenty to start. Don't wait to be in the mood; moods are like relatives from the provinces, who come when it suits them and leave when you have got used to them. Sit down, do your half hour, get up. If you wrote badly, you have something to correct tomorrow. A blank page can't be corrected at all.

Make the portion small enough for an invalid. I say this from experience. When I was writing that same play I was ill, and on many days I managed only a few lines. I complained about it in my letters, as you would have. But a few lines a day, put one after another, eventually came to four acts. I would not have believed it either.

Lower the ceiling. You tell me that your thesis must change the way people think about your subject. Who told it that it must? Let it be a decent, honest, moderately useful piece of work, with the dates correct. When I went to Sakhalin to see how the convicts lived, I came home with thousands of census cards and a head full of large intentions. The book took years. What got it finished was counting, checking, copying out, one plain chapter after another.

Stop talking about it. I have observed that work discussed at supper is seldom done at breakfast. Talking gives us the pleasant feeling of having accomplished something, along with the wine. For one month, when friends ask about the thesis, say "It's coming along," and change the subject to fish.

I'd also ask you to check the ordinary things before the profound ones. Are you sleeping? Do you eat before three in the afternoon? Is your room warm? I have met young men tormented by the question of their own worthlessness who turned out to need spectacles.

And be a little suspicious of your guilt. It looks like conscience, but it takes up the whole evening and produces nothing, which is exactly what you accuse yourself of. A man who has done his half hour may go fishing with a clear heart. A man who has spent the day reproaching himself gets neither the work nor the fish.

I am serious about one thing. If the trouble is wider than the thesis, if you can't make yourself wash or answer a friend, if nothing has given you pleasure for weeks, then it isn't idleness and no timetable will cure it. Go to a doctor and tell them plainly.

Otherwise, you know what to do. You have known for eleven pages. Please don't answer this letter until you have written one page of the other thing, and then let your answer be short.
