Epictetus to a student who cannot stop looking sideways.

You tell me that a friend of yours was promoted, and you smiled and congratulated him, and then went home and could not sleep. You tell me that you look at the lives of other people, their houses, their holidays, their happy families, and feel a sour taste that you are ashamed of. You ask me how to stop envying.

Good. You are ashamed, which means you know this feeling does you no good. That is where we begin.

Let me ask you the question I ask every student. What is up to you? Your judgments, your choices, what you want, what you avoid, how you treat people, how you do your work. What is not up to you? Your body, your reputation, other people's opinions, promotions, money, whether the world rewards you. That is the whole map. Most of our misery comes from living on the wrong side of it.

Now look at what you envy. Your friend's promotion. Is it up to you? No. Another man's house? No. Someone's happy marriage? No. You are tormenting yourself over things that were never in your hands. It is as if you stood at the edge of a field you do not own and wept because the crops are not yours.

I was born a slave. I had a master who could do with my body what he liked, and for a long time I owned nothing, not even myself in the eyes of the law. I had every reason, you might think, to envy. And at first I did. But I came to see that the freest man in the household was not always the one who held the keys. My master lived on the emperor's favour, and in the end that same favour cost him his life. I learned to be calm inside a chain. Many men in that palace were never calm at all. So tell me, who was poorer?

Here is the hard truth about anything you envy. Everything has a price. Your friend got his promotion by working evenings, by flattering certain people, by giving up time with his children. Perhaps it was worth it to him. Would it be worth it to you? If you want what he has, you must pay what he paid. If you will not pay, do not complain that you did not get it. You kept your coins. You cannot have both the coins and the goods.

So when envy rises, try this. Say to it: "This is only an impression. Let me look at it before I agree with it." Then ask: what exactly do I want here? Is it the thing itself, or is it the feeling of being admired? Very often it is the second. And admiration is the flimsiest thing in the world, here today and given to someone else tomorrow.

Then turn your eyes back to your own work. You have a character to build, choices to make well, people to treat fairly. No one can promote you in these things, and no one can take them from you. A man who is busy improving himself has little time to count other men's coins.

Stop scrolling through other people's lives as if they were a scoreboard. You are seeing their best days, polished. You are comparing them to your worst days, seen from the inside. It is a rigged game. Leave the table.

And if you want something, want it properly. Not "I want what he has," but "I want to become someone who could do that work well." The first is envy. The second is a goal, and goals are partly up to you.

Practise this for one month. Each time you feel the sour taste, name it, sort it, and return to what is yours. You will slip. Begin again. A student who falls and gets up is still on the road.

You have more than you think, because you have yourself: your choices, your judgments, your character. No promotion can add to that, and no rival can take it.
