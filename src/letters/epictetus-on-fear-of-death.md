Epictetus to a student who is afraid of dying.

You tell me you have become afraid of death. It began, you say, when a friend your age fell ill. Since then you notice every ache, read about every disease, and lie awake listening to your own heartbeat. You ask how a philosopher deals with this.

Good. You have brought me the largest question there is. Let us not waste it with comfortable words.

First, the old question. What is up to you, and what is not? Whether you will die is not up to you. You will. So will I, and so will everyone you have ever met. When you will die is also, for the most part, not up to you. You can eat sensibly, see your doctor, avoid foolish risks, and you should. After that, the date is not in your hands. So put it down. Holding it will not change it. It will only tire your arms.

What, then, is up to you? Your judgment about death. This is where all the trouble lives. What hurts you now, while you are perfectly alive, is the picture you have painted of it. Look at the picture. Is it dark, lonely, painful, unfair? Those are your words, not death's. Death has said nothing to you. You have been talking to yourself.

Try this. Write down your fear, exactly as you feel it. "I am afraid of being in pain." "I am afraid of leaving my children." "I am afraid of not existing." Now take each one and ask: is this a fact, or a judgment I have added? Pain at the end of life is possible, and it can often be eased by good physicians. Leaving your children is a real sorrow, and the answer to it is to love them well today. Not existing is the hardest of the three, and here I will only say this: you have no experience of it, only an idea, and an idea can be examined like any other.

I was born a slave. I walked with a limp. For years, my body belonged to another man by law. I learned early that a great many things could be taken from me without my consent. My body, my freedom of movement, my home. Later, the emperor drove the philosophers out of Rome, and I had to leave and begin teaching again in Nicopolis, in Greece. Every one of these losses taught me the same lesson: the things outside me were never firmly mine, and worrying over them did not help me keep them. What stayed with me through all of it was my own judgment and my own choices.

Your life is like this too. You did not earn it, and you cannot hold it by force. The question is not how long you will have it. The question is how you will use it while you do.

So here is your practice.

Each morning, when you wake, remind yourself plainly: "I have this day, and I will not have it again." Not to frighten yourself, but to make the day feel precious instead of ordinary. You will find you waste fewer hours on things you do not care about.

When you catch yourself imagining your death in detail, stop and ask: "Is this happening now?" It is not. You are sitting in a chair, breathing. Return to what is in front of you.

Turn your attention to how you are living. A man who lives well, treats people justly, and does his work properly has less reason to fear the end. He has not left his life undone. Most of our terror about death is really regret about life, dressed up.

And go easy on the reading. You will not reason your way out of this fear by studying every illness. Learn what you need for your health, act on it, and then close the book.

You will not master this in a week. I have practised for many years and still have moments of fear. But each time you separate the event from your judgment of it, the fear loses some of its grip.

If your fear becomes so strong that it fills your days and keeps you from sleeping or working, speak to a physician. Health anxiety is common, and it can be treated. That is also a choice that is up to you.

Begin today. Write the list, sort it, and keep only what is yours.
