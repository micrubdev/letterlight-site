My dear friend,

Your letter found me in my study in Concord, with the snow beginning outside. You tell me that you have left something behind: a career, a city, a way of life that everyone around you thought settled. Now you are standing at the start of something you cannot yet name, and you are frightened. Friends shake their heads. Relatives ask when you will come to your senses. You ask whether you have made a terrible mistake.

I cannot say whether your particular choice was wise. But I can tell you that I once stood where you stand, and that the step which looked to everyone like a fall turned out to be the beginning of my real work.

When I was not yet thirty, I was the minister of a church in Boston. It was a respectable position, the kind of thing my family had expected of me for generations. My young wife, Ellen, had died the year before, and I was grieving and uncertain. I found that I could no longer in good conscience perform one of the church's central rites as it was expected. I told the congregation so, and I resigned. Many thought I had thrown my life away.

I sailed for Europe soon after, ill and unsure of what I would become. I met writers I had admired, and in a garden of natural history in Paris I stood among the specimens and felt for the first time how much I wanted to understand the living world. When I came home, I began to lecture, and then to write. That was the start of everything I have done since.

So when you ask whether it is too late, or whether you were foolish to leave, I can only tell you this: an ending is often what makes room for the beginning you could not otherwise have reached.

Here is what I would counsel.

Listen to your own judgment. If I have taught anything, it is this. Somewhere inside you there is a quiet conviction about what you should do. It may be faint. It may be drowned out by the voices of sensible people. Listen for it anyway. Other people's advice is built from other people's lives. It may fit you, and it may not. Your own sense of things, however uncertain, was made for you alone.

Give yourself permission to change your mind. People will remind you of your old plans, your old opinions, the person you used to be. You are allowed to have learned something since then. Growing out of an old life is not a betrayal of it.

Spend time alone, and outdoors. I have walked the woods around Concord for years, and I have found there a steadiness I could not find in any parlour. Nature does not ask you to justify yourself. Go out among the trees and let your thoughts settle like silt in a still pond. You will see your situation more clearly afterwards.

Do the work in front of you. A new life is not built in a single bold leap. It is built in ordinary days of honest effort. Write the letter, take the class, knock on the door. Do not wait until you are certain. Certainty usually arrives after the work has begun, not before.

Expect to feel foolish. Every beginner does. My first little book sold slowly, and for years few people read it. I kept on, because the work felt like mine. That feeling is worth more than applause.

And look around at the world. Each spring the fields begin again. Each morning the sun returns as if for the first time. You belong to that same order. You are not finished. You are only between one circle and the next.

If your fear grows heavy enough to keep you from eating or sleeping, talk to a doctor or a trusted friend. Courage includes asking for help.

Write to me when you have taken your first step, and tell me what it felt like.
