My young friend,

You write that you have many good intentions and few finished things. You plan to study, to exercise, to write, and the plans stay plans. You know what you should do. You ask why you do not do it.

Let me begin with a story I am not proud of. One of my students, Tsai Yu, was clever and spoke well. One day I found him asleep in the middle of the day, when he should have been studying. I said that rotten wood cannot be carved, which was harsh. But I also learned something. Before that day, I listened to what people said and believed they would act on it. Afterwards I listened to what they said and watched what they did. Fine words are easy. The test is in the doing.

I do not tell you this to scold you. I tell you because you already know the difference between saying and doing, or you would not have written to me.

Think of learning, or any work, as building a mound of earth. If you need only one more basket to finish and you stop, the stopping is yours. No one else stopped you. And if you are standing on level ground with nothing built yet, and you throw down one basket, that one basket is real progress, and it is yours too. Both the stopping and the going forward belong to you.

This is good news. It means you do not need a great surge of will to change your life. You need a basket. One basket today. One tomorrow. A mound does not rise by a single heroic effort but by baskets carried on ordinary days, when nobody is watching.

Now let me say something about thinking. There was a minister in Lu called Chi Wan who always thought three times before he acted. People admired his care. When I heard of it, I said that twice would do. Thinking is necessary. But past a certain point, more thinking becomes a way of not acting. You weigh the choice again and again, you research and plan and prepare, and you feel busy, but nothing is built. If you have considered a thing twice and it still seems right, carry the basket.

Here is what I would suggest.

First, make your task smaller than your excuses. If you mean to study for an hour and keep failing, study for ten minutes. If you mean to write a chapter, write a paragraph. A small thing done each day is worth more than a large thing planned each night.

Second, attach your effort to a fixed hour. Ritual is not only for ceremonies. It is a way of making the right action easy to remember. Choose a time and a place for your basket, and keep to it as you would keep an appointment with a respected teacher.

Third, find a companion in the work. When I walk with two others, I can always find a teacher among them. Study with a friend, report your progress to someone you respect, or simply tell one person what you mean to do this week. We are more faithful to promises made aloud.

Fourth, do not wait to feel ready. A man who waits until he loves the work before beginning may wait forever. Often the love comes after the effort, not before it.

Fifth, when you fall behind, do not throw down the basket in disgust. One missed day is a missed day. Two missed days are the start of a habit. Return on the third morning, and begin again without fuss.

This week, choose one mound you have left unfinished. Each day, carry one basket to it, however small. On the seventh day, look at what you have built.

The going forward will be your own.
