Epictetus to a student who has lost his post.

You have come to me upset, so I will be blunt with you, and then I will be kind.

You lost your job. You tell me this as if it were the worst thing that could happen to a person. I was born a slave. My master once twisted my leg until it broke, and I walk with a limp to this day. I do not tell you this so that you will feel small. I tell you so that you will believe me when I say that a great deal can be taken from a person, and still the most important thing stays where it was.

So let us do what I make all my students do on the first day. Take a sheet and draw a line down the middle.

On one side, write what was never up to you. The company's profits. The manager who drew up the list. The economy, the market, the decision made in a meeting you were not invited to. Your reputation, which lives in other people's heads. Whether the next company calls you back. Write all of it down. Be thorough.

On the other side, write what is up to you. It is a shorter list, and that is good news, not bad. What you think about what happened. What you do tomorrow morning. How you speak to your family tonight. Whether you send the applications or sit refreshing your inbox. How honestly you look at your own part in it, and how quickly you stop looking once you have learned what there is to learn.

Now look at the sheet. You have spent this week on the left side, haven't you? Hours on the meeting, on the manager, on what your colleagues are saying. That is where the suffering comes from. Not from the loss itself, but from your judgment that it was unbearable, unfair, a disgrace. The job ended once. You have lost it again every hour since, in your head.

I used to ask my students to change one word when they talk about such things. Not lost. Given back. The job was never yours the way your mind is yours. It was lent to you, for a time, by people who could take it back. They took it back. You may be sad about that, as you would be sad to give back a good horse you had ridden for years. But you were not robbed. You were a borrower whose loan came due.

I hear you say: easy for a philosopher, but I have bills. Yes. And the bills belong on the right side of the sheet, because what you do about them is up to you. Sit down today and work out how many months you can last. Cut what you can. Ask for help where it is honestly needed. This is not philosophy against practical life. This is philosophy as practical life. A Stoic with a budget is still a Stoic.

You are also angry with yourself. You think you should have seen it coming, worked harder, made yourself impossible to let go. Perhaps there is something in that. Write it down once, plainly, as a lesson for next time. Then stop. A man who keeps punishing himself for a past he cannot change has made himself the slave of yesterday. I did not survive one master to watch you appoint yourself another.

Here is your practice for this week. Each morning, before you check anything, say to yourself: today I will be offered things that are not up to me, and I will leave them alone. Then do the three things on your list that are up to you. Each evening, ask: where did I give my peace away today, and to whom? You will catch yourself many times. Good. That is the training. Nobody becomes free in a day.

When the next job comes, hold it the same way. Do the work well, because doing it well is up to you. But hold it with an open hand. Whatever you hold with a closed fist, you will be afraid of losing.

Now go and work on the right side of the sheet.
