My dear friend,

You write that you have begun to lie awake thinking about death. Not anyone's in particular, simply your own. You say it comes at night, a cold wave, and you cannot argue it away. You ask me, of all people, what I think, since the Athenians once gave me a cup of poison and I drank it.

I am glad you wrote, though I warn you that I am a poor giver of answers. I will do what I always do, which is ask you questions, and we will see together where they lead.

So let me begin. When you say you fear death, what is it you fear? Is it the pain of dying? Is it the thought of no longer being? Is it leaving people behind, or leaving things undone? These are very different fears, and they may need different medicines. A man who fears the pain should talk to his physician. A man who fears leaving things undone should perhaps start doing them. Which is yours? Perhaps it is all of them, tangled together. Then let us try to untangle them one at a time, the way you would separate threads.

Now a harder question. Do you know what death is?

I ask because when I was on trial, and the jury was deciding whether I should live, I found that I did not know. I had spent my life discovering how little I knew, and death turned out to be one more thing on that long list. It seemed to me then that there were only two possibilities. Either death is a kind of dreamless sleep, and if you think of the best night's sleep you ever had, the one with no dreams at all, you may agree that this would not be so terrible. Or death is a journey to another place, where perhaps we may meet those who went before us and go on talking. And if that were so, I said, I would gladly spend eternity asking questions of the wise, and finding out which of them were wise and which only thought they were.

I do not tell you that either of these is true. I tell you only that I could not prove death was bad. And if I cannot prove it is bad, is it reasonable to be so terrified of it? We are afraid of many things we have never examined. Death may be the largest of them.

Here is another question, and it is the one I care about most. How are you living?

You see, I have noticed that the people most tormented by death are often the ones who feel they have not yet lived. They are waiting to begin. Their real life is always next year. Death frightens them because it threatens to arrive before the beginning. If this is you, the cure may not be to think more about death but to think more about your days. What would you be doing if you were not afraid? Whom would you speak to? What would you stop wasting time on?

When my friends came to me in prison and offered to help me escape, I asked them to reason it through with me, as we always had. I did not want to spend my last days running from death and abandoning everything I had argued for. I would rather spend them as I had spent the rest of my life, talking with friends about how to live well. That was, I suppose, my answer to death. It was not a doctrine. It was a way of spending the hours I had.

So I will leave you with a small task, which is the only kind I trust. Tonight, when the cold wave comes, do not argue with it. Ask it questions. What are you? What do you want me to do? Is your fear about the end, or about the middle? Then write down what it answers.

And if the fear grows so large that it keeps you from sleeping night after night, or from living by day, speak to a physician or a counsellor. Some fears need company to examine.

You may find, as I did, that the unexamined fear is far larger than the examined one.
