Dear friend,

Your letter came on a grey morning, and I read it twice. You say you are used up. You work late and wake tired, the things you once cared about feel like chores, and you no longer remember why you started. You ask how a person gets back to themselves.

I know something of this. As a young man I was a minister in Boston, the work my family had done for generations. My young wife had died, my health was poor, and I came to believe that the Lord's Supper, which my church asked me to give, was not meant to be a permanent rite. I left the pulpit. Some thought I had thrown away a career. I went to Europe to recover, and came home to a different kind of work. So I will not tell you to try harder. Trying harder is what brought you here.

Here is what I would tell you instead. Go outside.

I do not say this as a pleasant suggestion. I say it because the woods did more for me than any sermon. A man goes into the trees carrying his whole office on his back, the unanswered letters and the disappointed faces, and after an hour he finds he has set it down somewhere without noticing. The fields do not ask you to perform. The pines are not keeping score. Standing on the bare ground with the open sky above you, the little fretful self that has been running your days grows quiet, and something larger and steadier speaks up. Call it reason, or faith, or simply your own good sense. It has been there all along, drowned out by the noise.

You may say you have no woods nearby and no hours to spare. Then start smaller. Walk to work by the long way. Eat your lunch on a bench under a tree instead of at your desk. Watch the light change at sunset for ten minutes without a screen in your hand. Nature is generous and asks very little of us. A patch of sky between two buildings will do to begin with.

The second thing is harder. Look honestly at what you are carrying and ask whose load it is. Much of what exhausts us is not our own work but our idea of what other people expect. We say yes because a good employee says yes, a good son or daughter says yes, a good friend says yes. Each yes is small. Together they bury us. I have come to believe that a person who trusts himself will find his own work lighter than another man's, even when it is heavier, because he does it from the inside. So make a list of your duties and mark the ones you would choose if no one were watching. Then start, gently, to put down the others. You will disappoint some people. They will survive it, and so will you.

Third, rest without apology. Our age praises the busy man as if busyness were a virtue. It is often only a way of hiding from ourselves. Sleep is no laziness. A day with nothing in it is not wasted. The farmer leaves a field fallow so that it will bear again. You are allowed the same.

Fourth, remember what you loved before you were tired. Was it music? Building things? Long talks with a friend? Burnout often means we have spent all our hours on what we must do and none on what feeds us. Put one hour back this week. Treat it as an appointment you cannot break.

And if the tiredness does not lift after rest, if your sleep and appetite are gone or the days look dark, see a physician. A tired mind can also be an ill one, and there is no shame in getting it looked after.

Tonight, before you sleep, step outside for five minutes. Look up. Notice one thing in the sky or the trees or the street that you would have hurried past yesterday. Write it down. Do the same tomorrow.

Each morning is a fresh start, and you do not have to carry yesterday's weariness into it. The woods will wait for you, and they will not ask where you have been.
