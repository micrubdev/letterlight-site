Seneca greets his Lucilius.

You confess, with the honesty I have always liked in you, that you are a champion at putting things off. The letter you meant to write, the work you promised yourself, the change you have been planning since spring: all of it waits for a better day. You tell me that you are not lazy. You are busy all day long. You simply never get to the things you care about most.

I know this disease well, because I have had it. For years I told myself that once my affairs were in order I would give my time to philosophy and to my friends. My affairs were never in order. It was only late in life, after I stepped back from the court, that I began to write to you as I do now. I do not regret the letters. I regret the years I spent promising them.

Let me tell you what I have noticed about putting things off. The task we delay is almost never hard because of the work itself. It is hard because of what it means. The letter is hard because it might be answered coldly. The book is hard because it might be bad. The conversation is hard because it might go wrong. We are not avoiding the work. We are avoiding the fear that sits on top of it. And so we fill the day with small, safe tasks that let us feel useful without ever risking anything.

There is a second trick the mind plays. It treats tomorrow as if it were a different person, one with more energy, more courage and more free time, who will gladly do what we will not. But tomorrow's man is only you again, with the same fears and one day less.

So here is my advice, and it is not complicated.

Make the first step absurdly small. Do not sit down to "write the book." Sit down to write one paragraph, badly. Do not resolve to "sort out your affairs." Open one letter and answer it. The fear grows huge when we look at the whole mountain. It shrinks when we only look at the next stone.

Do the important thing first, before the day gets hold of you. The morning is the most valuable part of the day, and most people spend it answering other people's requests. Give the first hour to your own work, even if it is only half an hour, even if it is only ten minutes. The rest of the day can have what is left.

Keep an account of your time for a week, as a merchant keeps an account of his money. Write down where each hour went. You will be embarrassed, as I was. You will find hours that slipped away on nothing, in idle talk, in waiting, in worrying about the very task you were not doing. Once you see the leak, you can mend it.

Forgive yourself for yesterday. Many people who put things off then waste more time feeling guilty about it, which is simply another way of not starting. Guilt is useful for a moment, as a nudge. After that it is only one more excuse. Yesterday is gone. You cannot spend it twice, and you cannot get it back by mourning it. Today you still have.

And remember what all this is for. Of everything we have, time is what we hand away most carelessly. People who would never let a stranger take a coin from their purse let anyone take an afternoon. If you do not decide what your days are for, others will decide for you, and they will not choose your work.

I am not asking you to become a machine that never rests. Rest is part of a good life. I am asking you to stop postponing the life you meant to have.

Write to me tomorrow, not next week, and tell me what you did with your first hour.
