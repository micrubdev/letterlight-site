Greetings, my dear friend.

You tell me that your mother can no longer live alone, and that you have become her nurse, her driver, her cook and her keeper of pills, while still doing your own work and raising your own children. You love her. You are also exhausted, and some evenings you feel a flash of resentment that frightens you. You ask whether this makes you a bad son.

It makes you a tired one. Let us begin there, because guilt and fatigue look very similar by lamplight, and you have mistaken one for the other.

I owe a great deal to the women who cared for me. When I was a child, it was my aunt who carried me in her arms to Rome, and it was her nursing that brought me back to strength after a long sickness. Much later, when I was ill again as a young man, I was kept going by friends who sat at my bedside and talked with me. I learned then what the sick need most. They need the medicine, of course, and the meals on time. But they need, more than anything, the sense that someone is glad to be with them. Remember this when you count your failures. You may forget a prescription, and the doctor can fix that. What your mother will remember is whether you were there.

So, first, set aside the idea that you must do everything. A soldier who refuses to sleep because the watch matters so much will fall asleep on duty. You serve your mother better by guarding your own strength. Ask your brothers and sisters for specific help, not vague offers: Tuesday afternoons, the pharmacy, one weekend in four. Find out what the town offers. There are people paid to care, and paying them is not abandoning her. It may be the very thing that lets you visit as her son and not only as her servant.

Second, do not be ashamed of the resentment. Anger at an endless burden is natural. The mind rebels against what it did not choose. What matters is what you do with the feeling. Write it down, say it to a friend, walk it off around the block. Do not let it pile up in silence until it spills onto her. Feelings that are spoken lose half their weight.

Third, keep something for yourself. I have written to you before that time is the one thing truly ours, and that we let others take it too easily. In your case you are giving it willingly, and nobly. But keep back an hour each week that belongs to no one: a walk, a book, a meal with a friend. Call it maintenance. A lamp that is never refilled goes out.

Fourth, accept that she is changing, and that you cannot stop it. Much of a caregiver's pain comes from fighting the decline as if diligence could reverse it. Do what is in your power, and then let the rest belong to nature. Old age comes to everyone who is lucky enough to reach it. Your task is to make the road gentler, not to turn it back.

Fifth, look for the gifts that are still there. She may repeat herself. She may not know what day it is. But there may also be an old story you have never heard, a song she still remembers, a joke at the nurse's expense. Write these down. Later you will be glad you did.

If you find you cannot sleep, cannot eat, or have stopped caring about anything at all, see a physician for yourself. Carers fall ill more often than people think, and you cannot carry her if you collapse.

This week, then, do two things. Ask one person for one specific piece of help, and name the day. And spend ten minutes with your mother doing nothing useful at all: just sitting, talking, or listening to something she likes.

You are giving back a little of what was once given to you. Give it gently, and include yourself among those you care for.
