Dear friend,

So the position is gone. You were called into a room, told kindly or not so kindly that you were no longer needed, and sent home with a box. You write that you feel ashamed, as though everyone can see it on your face, and that you do not know who you are without the title on your door.

Let me say first that I am sorry. It is a blow, and you are entitled to feel it.

But I will not pretend I think it is the end of anything. I have watched too many people mistake one door closing for the whole house falling down. Young men come to Boston or New York, fail in their first venture, and decide they are ruined. Their friends agree with them. They spend the rest of their lives complaining. Meanwhile a farm boy from Vermont tries one trade after another, teaching school, peddling, farming, editing a newspaper, and each time he falls he lands on his feet like a cat. He does not think of himself as having failed at a profession. He thinks of himself as a man who works. I would rather be that boy than a hundred of the others.

You are more than the job you held. The job was a coat you wore. It fitted, perhaps, and kept you warm, and now it is gone. The person who wore it is still here, with the same hands and the same mind and the same capacity for work.

I speak from some experience. When I was not yet thirty I gave up the pulpit, the work I had trained for. I had a family name to live up to and no clear idea what to do next. I began giving lectures, and the first ones were not even on the subjects I would later be known for. They were on natural science, because that is where I could find an audience. Out of those small beginnings came a working life I could not have planned. I did not know that when I walked out of the church. I only knew I had to do the next honest thing.

So here is what I would tell you.

First, do not let other people's pity decide how you see yourself. Some will look at you as if you were wounded. You need not agree. Their opinion is their affair. Your task is to keep your own counsel and trust what you know of your own worth.

Second, make a list of everything you can do. Not your job title, which is a narrow thing, but your actual abilities. Can you organise? Persuade? Fix machines? Teach? Write a clear letter? Calm an angry customer? You have likely done ten kinds of work under one name. Any of them may be the next road.

Third, give yourself a short season to grieve and then set a time to begin. A week of rest is sensible. A season of hiding is not. Rise at your usual hour. Dress as if you had somewhere to be, because you do: the work of finding work.

Fourth, talk to people. Not only those who might hire you, but anyone doing work you find interesting. Ask how they came to it. Most will be glad to tell you, and nearly every path you hear about will turn out to have been less straight than it looks from outside.

Fifth, be willing to try something that seems beneath you or beside you. The Vermont boy did not wait for the perfect position. He took the next one and learned from it. A temporary job is not a verdict on your life.

If the money is short and the worry keeps you awake, attend to that plainly. Make a budget, find out what help you are owed, and tell the people you live with the truth. Fear grows in silence and shrinks when it is spoken.

Tomorrow, write that list of what you can do. Make it long. Then pick one item and find one person to talk to about it this week.

Trust yourself. You have landed on your feet before, in smaller ways, and you will again.
