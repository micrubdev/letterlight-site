My dear colleague in loneliness,

Your letter reached me at a dreary hour, when the snow was coming down sideways and I had been coughing all afternoon, so I read it with great sympathy and a cup of tea. You say that you are lonely, that the evenings are long, and that you have begun to feel sorry for yourself and then ashamed of feeling sorry for yourself. You ask what a doctor would prescribe.

As a doctor, I will start with the body, because the body is always where I start. When were you last outside in daylight? When did you last walk for an hour? What did you eat today, and was it eaten standing up over the sink? Loneliness and a poor diet are old friends, and they make each other worse. Sleep at a regular hour. Eat soup. Go outside every day, even when it is grey. I am not being flippant. I have seen many melancholy patients improve on fresh air and porridge before anything else.

Now the prescription for the soul, which is less exact.

First, a small dose of honesty. You write that you feel sorry for yourself. That is natural, and you may do it for ten minutes a day, no more. Set a clock if you must. Self-pity is a comfortable chair that becomes very hard to get out of. After ten minutes, stand up and do something useful, however small. Wash the dishes. Mend the coat. Write a page. Work is the best medicine I know for low spirits, and I have tried most of them.

Second, talk to people, even briefly, even badly. You need not make friends at once. Speak to the porter, the man who sells newspapers, the woman in the flat below who always seems to be carrying too many bags. Ask how they are and listen to the answer. In the country I learned that the shortest conversation with a peasant at the side of the road could warm a whole day. People are lonely everywhere, and most of them are waiting for someone to speak first.

Third, be of use to someone. When I was most miserable, the cure was nearly always to go and see a patient, or to help my brothers with a problem, or to plant trees in the garden at Melikhovo. Loneliness turns us inward until we can see nothing but ourselves. Helping someone else turns us outward again. Volunteer somewhere. Teach something you know. Visit an old person who has fewer visitors than you do. You will find, I think, that when you are needed, you are rarely lonely.

Fourth, and this is the one I believe in most: remember that you are connected to people you will never meet. I once wrote a story about a student walking home on a cold night, gloomy and sure that life was nothing but cold and hunger forever. He stopped to tell a story to two widows at a fire, and saw them moved to tears by something that happened nineteen centuries ago. Suddenly he felt that all of it was joined together, the past and the present, them and him. I think that is true for you as well. The people who wrote the books you love, the strangers who built your street, the person reading the same page somewhere else tonight: you are not as separate as the evening tells you.

And if the loneliness turns into something darker, if you find you can no longer get up in the morning or see any point in anything, then go to a doctor at once. Not a writer: a doctor, a real one, who can examine you properly. Melancholy of that kind is an illness like any other and should be treated like one.

Write to me again in a month and tell me how the porridge is going.
