My friend,

Your letter tells me that the life you built has come apart, and that you must now begin again at an age when you expected to be settled. You feel foolish, you say, like a grown man sitting down among children to learn the alphabet. You ask whether it is too late.

Sit down. Let us talk about this slowly, because it matters.

I know something about beginning with very little. I came to Jerusalem from Babylonia as a poor man, with no name and no money. The stories people tell about me say I was already forty years old when I began to study in earnest. I cannot vouch for every number in those stories, but I can tell you that I was no youth, and that I sat among students who knew far more than I did.

They also tell a story about a winter Friday when I had no coin to pay the doorkeeper of the house of study. I climbed onto the roof and lay beside the skylight so I could hear the teachers below, and the snow fell on me until I was found in the morning, half frozen. Perhaps it happened as they say, perhaps not. But the feeling in it is true. When you want to learn badly enough, you will find a way to listen, even from the roof.

So, is it too late? Let me answer with some questions of my own.

Is there breath in you? Then it is not too late. The teachers I loved most did not count a person's years. They looked at whether he was willing to sit down and learn.

Are you ashamed to be a beginner? Put that shame away. A person who is too proud to ask cannot learn, and a teacher who is too impatient cannot teach. You may need to ask simple questions, again and again. Ask them. There is no dishonour in not knowing. The dishonour is in pretending.

Are you waiting for a better season? Seasons do not improve by waiting. If you need more money, more strength, more clarity before you start, start with the little you have, and let the work bring you the rest. A poor man who studies a little each day will, in time, know more than a rich man who plans to study someday.

Now, here is something I would add, because starting over can make a person turn inward. When your own life has fallen apart, it is natural to think only of yourself: your losses, your plans, your fears. You must care for yourself, certainly. No one else will build your new life for you. But do not let the rebuilding make you small. Look around. Who else is starting over? Who could use a word of encouragement, a meal, an hour of your time? You will find that helping another person begin again gives you strength for your own beginning. I have seen this many times.

Be gentle with yourself as you learn. I was known, I am told, for being hard to anger. People made wagers that they could provoke me, and lost their money. I do not say this to boast. Patience did not come to me by nature. I practised it, as one practises a trade. You will need patience now, mostly with yourself. You will make mistakes, forget things, feel slow. Answer yourself the way you would answer a child who is trying hard: kindly, and with encouragement to try again.

Keep company with people who are learning. Find a teacher, a class, a group, a friend who is also starting something. Do not try to do it all alone in your room. Learning is a conversation. Even the greatest scholars of my time argued with each other in order to see more clearly.

And take one step today. Not a great one. Make one telephone call, write one page, sign up for one class, clear one shelf. Tomorrow, take another. A long journey is walked the same way a short one is, step by step, and the person who sits waiting for the road to shorten never arrives.

You told me you feel foolish. I think you are brave. It takes more courage to begin again in the middle of life than to begin at the start, when everyone is a beginner.

Write and tell me what step you took today.
