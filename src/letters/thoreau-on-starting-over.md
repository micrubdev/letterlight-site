Dear friend,

Your letter came on a grey morning, and I read it on a walk by the pond. You tell me that your old life has ended. The job is gone, or the marriage, or the city you lived in, and you are standing in the middle of what is left, not sure whether to grieve or to begin. You ask how one starts over at your age.

I am no expert in starting over, but I did it once on purpose, and the experiment taught me more than all my years at college.

When I was twenty-seven I took a borrowed axe to a stand of white pines beside Walden Pond, on a woodlot my friend Mr. Emerson owned, and started cutting timber for a small house. I moved in that July. I had tried a few ways of living by then. I had taught school and left it, worked in my father's pencil business, and written essays few people read. My neighbours thought me idle. Perhaps I was, by their measure. But I wanted to find out what life actually required, and whether I could meet those requirements and have time left over for living.

I stayed a little over two years. Then I left, because the experiment had taught me what it could, and I did not want it to harden into one more habit.

Here is the first thing I learned, and I offer it to you. Most of what we carry, we carry out of habit. We acquire a house, then furniture to fill the house, then work to pay for the furniture, and soon we are spending our only life tending possessions we hardly use. When your old life ends, it can feel like a robbery. It may also be an unexpected chance to put down what you never truly needed.

So begin by making an inventory. Not of what you have lost, but of what you have left. Walk through your rooms, your days, your obligations, and ask of each thing: do I want this, or did I merely inherit it? Keep what you want. Let the rest go, gradually if you must.

The second thing I learned is that a new life is built from very small pieces. I did not build my house in a day. I cut timbers, I bought an old shanty for its boards, I dug a cellar in the sandy hillside. Each task was plain, and most of it was dull. But at the end of each day there was a little more house than there had been in the morning. You do not need to know what your whole new life will look like. You need only to cut today's timber.

The third thing is to go outdoors. I do not say this lightly. When I could not think, I walked, and the woods did a good deal of my thinking for me. Watch how the pond freezes and thaws. Watch how a burned field comes back green in a single season. Nature starts over every spring without apology, and there is something in us that remembers how.

Be careful of advice, mine included. Your neighbours, your relations, your old colleagues will all have opinions about what you ought to do next. Most of them will recommend some version of what they themselves did. Listen politely. Then go somewhere quiet and ask what you actually want. If you find yourself out of step with the people around you, that may be a sign you are listening to yourself at last. Keep your own pace.

And do not wait for courage before you begin. I was not especially brave when I went to the pond. I was curious. Curiosity is easier to find than courage, and it will get you nearly as far. Ask yourself, "What would I like to find out about my life?" and then go and find it out.

There will be days when starting over feels like failure dressed up in hopeful words. On those days, simplify further. Eat something plain. Sleep. Walk. Do one honest piece of work. Then let the day end. Tomorrow is another chance to begin, and there are more such days in a life than we suppose.

Whatever you lost, you still have your hours, and they are yours to spend. You may be richer this year than you think.

Write and tell me what you have decided to keep.
