Seneca greets his Lucilius.

You confess something you say you have told no one else: that you are lonely. You have colleagues, acquaintances, people who greet you warmly in the street. But there is no one you could call at midnight, no one who knows what you are really thinking. You fill your evenings with noise and still feel alone. You ask what a philosopher would say about this.

First, I thank you for trusting me with it. Loneliness is a thing people hide, as if it were a disgrace. It is not. It is a sign that you were made for company, as all of us were.

I have known loneliness of the plainest kind. For eight years I lived in exile on Corsica, sent away from Rome, from my family and my friends, to a rocky island where I knew almost no one. I wrote to my mother to console her, and in consoling her I tried to console myself. I learned there that a person can be cut off from almost everything and still have two companions: his own mind and the friendship of those far away, kept alive by letters. I learned too that solitude can be borne, and even used, if one does not let it turn into self-pity.

Let me tell you what I have come to think about friendship, because I believe that is where your remedy lies.

Many people want friends for what friends can do for them. Someone to listen to their troubles, to help them when they are ill, to fill an empty evening. There is nothing shameful in wanting this. But a friendship built only on what we can receive is fragile. It is a kind of trade, and trades end when one side stops paying.

The better kind of friendship begins from the other direction. You look for someone to whom you can be useful. You become the one who remembers the birthday, who turns up when things go wrong, who listens without hurrying to speak. Such friendships grow slowly, but they hold. And there is a strange benefit: the person who sets out to give friendship rarely stays lonely for long.

So here is what I would advise.

Reach out first. Do not wait to be invited. Write to someone you have lost touch with. Ask a colleague to share a meal. People are often as lonely as you are and equally afraid to make the first move. Someone must be brave. Let it be you.

Choose well. Not everyone who is pleasant to talk to will make a true friend. Look for people whose character you admire, who are honest with you, who improve you a little by their company. Spend more time with them and less with those who leave you feeling empty.

Be patient. A true friend is not made in a week. Meet again, and again. Share small things before large ones. Trust grows at its own pace, like a tree, and cannot be hurried.

Learn to be good company for yourself. This is not a substitute for friends, but it makes the waiting easier. Read something worth reading. Walk. Write letters, as I do. A person who is at peace in his own company brings more to others and is less desperate in his search for them.

Serve others even before you have friends. Volunteer, teach, help a neighbour. Being useful connects you to people, and it reminds you that you have something to give.

Do not judge yourself harshly in the meantime. You are not lonely because you are unworthy of friendship. You are lonely because friendship takes time and effort to build, and life has not yet given you the chance.

If your loneliness has become so heavy that you feel hopeless, or you have stopped eating or sleeping properly, please talk to a physician. That is a reasonable and sensible thing to do.

Write to me this week, and tell me whom you have written to.
