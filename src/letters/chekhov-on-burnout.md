My dear friend,

Your letter made me laugh once and then made me worried, which is a fair summary of most letters I receive. You describe your days: up before light, work until evening, then the household, then messages until midnight, then lying awake thinking about tomorrow's work. You say you are proud of how much you do, and also that you have begun to forget words, snap at your family, and feel nothing at weekends except dread of Monday. You ask whether this is simply what adult life is.

Speaking as a doctor, which I am, I must tell you: no. What you describe is a set of symptoms, and symptoms are the body's way of writing a complaint.

I know something about overwork. When I was a medical student in Moscow, my father's business had failed, and the family looked to me. I wrote comic sketches for the magazines at night, dozens of them, sometimes several a week, to pay for food and rent. I treated patients and wrote stories at the same time, and later, when cholera threatened our district, I took charge of a medical section and drove from village to village. I coughed and ignored it, because there was always something more urgent. I do not recommend my example. I was young and foolish about my own health, as doctors often are.

In my play about Uncle Vanya, a young woman named Sonia comforts her uncle at the end. They have worked all their lives for someone else's comfort, and she tells him they will go on working, patiently, and that they will rest only after death. It is a moving speech, and I wrote it. But I did not write it as advice. It is a portrait of two good people who were never taught that their own lives mattered too.

So let me prescribe, as I would for any patient.

Sleep comes first. You cannot repay months of short nights in one weekend, but you can begin. Go to bed at the same hour every night for two weeks, even if you lie there. Put the telephone in another room. Your body knows how to recover if you stop interrupting it.

Take one whole day each week when you do no work at all. Not a day of catching up on housework, not a day of answering just a few messages. A day for walking, eating slowly, sitting in a garden, reading something that has nothing to do with your job. Mark it on the calendar like an appointment with a specialist.

Make a list of everything you are responsible for, and look at it with a cold eye. Some of these things are truly yours. Some you took on because no one else did, or because you could not say no. Choose one to hand back this month. People will survive. They usually manage better than we think.

Lower your standards a little. I have known many good, conscientious people who worked themselves ill because they could not bear to do anything imperfectly. Most tasks need to be done adequately, not beautifully. Save your best effort for the few things that deserve it.

Notice small pleasures. When I bought a little estate in the country, I found enormous happiness in planting trees, watching the fish in the pond, and walking with my dogs in the evening. None of it was important. All of it helped. Your pleasures may be different. Find them and protect them.

And laugh when you can. A tired person loses the ability to see that most of life is absurd. The meeting that felt like a catastrophe is, from a distance, a comedy. Try to see it from a distance.

If after a few weeks of rest you still feel empty, cannot sleep, or find no pleasure in anything, please see a doctor. That may be depression, and it is an illness, not a weakness. Treat it as you would a fever.

Write to me after your first day off and tell me what you did with it. If the answer is "nothing at all," I shall be delighted.
