Beloved,

Your letter arrived like a bird at the window, tired from a long flight. You tell me that the life you knew has ended. The house is sold, or the love has gone, or the work that gave your days their shape is finished. You stand in an empty room and wonder who you are now. You ask how anyone begins again.

Come, sit with me a while. I have had to begin again more than once, and I will tell you what I know.

When I was a boy, my family left our home in Balkh, far to the east, and travelled for years across many lands before we settled in Konya. I remember little of the old house, but I remember the feeling of leaving: the way a child looks back at a road until the last roof disappears. Everything familiar was behind us. Ahead was only dust and strangers. And yet in Konya I grew up, studied, became a teacher like my father, and had students of my own. The road that took everything from us also brought us to where we needed to be.

Later, when I was a respected scholar with a settled life, a wandering dervish named Shams came to Konya, and nothing was the same again. Our friendship turned my learning upside down. I had been a man of books and sermons. Through him I became something I had no name for. Then he vanished, and I was left with a grief so large I did not know where to put it. I searched for him. I could not find him. And out of that searching, the poems began to pour, more than I could ever have planned.

So I have learned that endings and beginnings are not two separate things. They are one movement, like breathing out and breathing in. What feels to you like a door slammed shut may be a door swinging open on its other side. You cannot see that side yet. That is all.

Here is what I would ask of you, gently.

First, let yourself grieve what is gone. Do not hurry past it to be brave. Your sorrow is a measure of how much you loved that life, and love should not be rushed out the door.

Second, notice that you are already changing. The person who wrote to me is not quite the person who lived in that old life. Even while you feel stuck, something in you is moving. Trust that movement, even before you understand it.

Third, keep your hands busy and your heart open. Make bread. Sweep the floor. Walk in the evening. Speak to a stranger in the market. New life rarely arrives through grand decisions. It slips in through small doors you happened to leave unlocked.

Fourth, be willing to become a beginner. When Shams came, I had to set aside much of what I thought I knew. It was humbling. It was also the beginning of everything that mattered most in my life. If you insist on being the person you used to be, you leave no room for the person you are becoming.

Fifth, do not be alarmed by how your moods change. One day you will feel hopeful, the next you will miss the old life terribly, and sometimes a sudden happiness will make you feel guilty. All of this belongs to beginning again. None of it means you are doing it wrong.

I will not pretend this is easy. There will be nights when the old life calls to you so sweetly that you would give anything to go back. On those nights, remember that the old life also had its hard days, which memory has kindly softened. The new one will have its sweet days too. You have simply not reached them yet.

If the darkness grows so heavy that you cannot rise for days, please find someone to sit with you: a friend, a physician, a wise counsellor. Even the strongest traveller needs company on a hard road.

Write to me again when you notice the first new thing growing in you, however small.
