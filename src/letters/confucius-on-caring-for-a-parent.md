To a devoted son or daughter who is very tired,

Your letter reached me, and I read in it both love and exhaustion. Your mother is growing old. She forgets things, she needs help with tasks she once did without thinking, and she is sometimes sharp with you in a way she never used to be. You work all day and care for her in the evenings. You ask how to honour your parent without losing yourself.

First, let me say that the question does you credit. Many people care for a parent only in body. They bring food and pay bills, but their faces are cold and their voices impatient. You are asking how to do it well. That is already the heart of the matter.

I lost my own father when I was very small, and my mother raised me in poor circumstances. I learned many humble skills because we needed them. I did not have the chance to care for my father in his old age, and I have thought often about what that care should look like. Let me offer you what I have come to believe.

Providing for a parent is the beginning, not the whole. Food, medicine and a safe room matter greatly. But a parent can be fed and still feel like a burden. What turns support into respect is the manner in which it is given. A gentle face, a patient tone, a moment of real attention while you help her dress: these are harder than any errand, and they matter more. If you can only manage one, give fewer services with a warm face rather than more services with a cold one.

When she is difficult, remember who she was. She is not only the frail person in front of you. She is also the woman who carried you, fed you, worried over your fevers and your schooling. Sometimes, when her temper flares, it helps to remember one ordinary kindness she showed you long ago. Let that memory stand between you and your irritation.

When you disagree with her, speak gently. If she refuses a doctor or insists on doing something unsafe, you may need to persuade her. Do it with respect, and more than once if necessary. Do not argue as you would with a rival. If she will not be moved, find another way to keep her safe, and keep your affection steady.

Now, the part you may not expect from me. You must also look after yourself. A lamp that burns all its oil goes out, and then there is no light for anyone. If you are sleeping badly, eating poorly and snapping at your own children, your care will suffer. Rest is not disloyalty. It is part of doing your duty well over the long years this may take.

Share the burden. Are there brothers, sisters, cousins who could help? Speak to them plainly. Ask for specific help: one evening a week, one weekend a month, one bill paid. Families often fall into the habit of letting one person carry everything. Breaking that habit is not selfish. It allows everyone to fulfil their duty, not only you.

Accept help from outside the family too, if it is available. A nurse, a day centre, a neighbour who sits with her for an afternoon. In my day, a household relied on many hands. You should not be ashamed to rely on more than your own two.

Keep small rituals. Take tea together at the same hour. Read her a letter. Sit with her in the evening light. When memory weakens, routine gives comfort. And these quiet moments will be what you remember most, later.

Finally, pay attention to what she still enjoys. Perhaps it is a certain song, a dish from her childhood, news of the neighbours, the feel of sun on her hands. These pleasures grow fewer with age, so notice them while they are there, and make room for them often.

If the strain becomes too much, if you feel hopeless or begin to resent her deeply, speak to a physician or counsellor about yourself. Caring for the carer is also proper conduct.

The road is long, but every patient act along it is a kind of learning.
