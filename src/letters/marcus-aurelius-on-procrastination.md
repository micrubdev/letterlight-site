To a friend who stays in bed,

You write that each morning you wake, see the day waiting, and pull the blanket back over your head. You lie there making plans you do not keep. When you finally rise, the morning is half gone, and you spend the rest of the day chasing it. You ask how a man who governed an empire managed to get out of bed.

I will tell you a secret. I did not always manage it. I wrote notes to myself about this very problem, because I needed them. Some of those notebooks were kept in army camps along the Danube, where the mornings are cold and the reports never stop. In them you will find me arguing with myself about the blanket. The blanket is warm. The world is cold and full of difficult people. It is the most natural thing in the world to want to stay where you are.

But here is the question I put to myself, and now put to you. What were you made for? Not to lie under a blanket. The sun does not stay in bed. Neither does the river. Each thing in nature does its own work. Why would you refuse yours?

I know this sounds stern, so let me be practical as well.

Decide the night before what your first task will be. Not ten tasks: one. When you wake, you will not have to choose, only begin. Much of the misery of the morning is the weight of too many choices pressing on a sleepy mind.

Do not wait to feel like it. You will rarely feel like it. Feelings follow action more often than they lead it. Stand up first, and let the willingness catch up with you on the way to the door. If you wait for desire to arrive before you move, you will wait a long time.

Break the work into pieces so small that refusing them would be silly. You do not need to finish the whole thing today. You need to do the next thing, properly and calmly, as if it were the last task of your life. Then the next. A great deal of good work is done this way, one piece at a time, by people who never once felt ready.

Watch the stories you tell yourself about the task. "This will be awful." "I will fail at it." "Everyone will see how little I know." These are judgments, not facts. The task itself is just a task: some words, some figures, a conversation. It is your opinion of it that weighs so much. Set the opinion down and look at the plain thing in front of you. It is usually smaller than you feared.

And remember how short the time is. I have buried emperors and friends, and many of them left things undone that they always meant to do. You do not have endless mornings. This one, the one you are lying in now, is one of a limited number. It would be a pity to spend too many of them under a blanket, avoiding the very work that would give your days their meaning.

Do not punish yourself for the mornings you have already lost. That is only another way of staying in bed. Simply begin again, today, with one task.

If, however, you find that you cannot get up for weeks at a time, that nothing seems worth doing and sleep brings no rest, then speak to a physician. That is not a failure of will. It may be an illness, and it deserves care like any other.

Tomorrow, when you wake, remind yourself what the day is for. Then put your feet on the floor before you can argue.
