Dear friend,

You write that your mother asked you the same question eleven times on Sunday, and that on the eleventh you shouted. She did not remember it an hour later. You have remembered it every night since. You say you moved her into your home out of love, and that you now catch yourself listening at her door with something that is not love at all. You want to know what kind of person that makes you.

It makes you a tired one. I would be more worried if you had written to tell me that you were a saint.

I never had the chance to care for my own parents when they were old. My mother died before I was two, and I have no memory of her face. My father died when I was still a boy. I was brought up by aunts, and the dearest of them, Tatyana, was only a distant relation. What I know of affection I learned first from her. She grew old at Yasnaya Polyana while I was busy with my books and my family. I had a great deal of work in those years, and I thought it was very important. Now I would give up a good many of those pages for another evening in her room.

So I envy you a little, and I know how that must sound to a person who was up four times in the night.

There is a short tale I put into a reading book for children. An old grandfather's hands shake, and he spills his soup and breaks his dish, so his son and daughter-in-law stop seating him at the table and feed him behind the stove. When he breaks a dish there, the daughter-in-law says that from now on he will eat from a tub. One day they see their little boy fitting some bits of wood together on the floor. They ask what he is making. A tub, he says, to feed you from when you are old. They weep, and bring the grandfather back to the table. I did not invent it; it is much older than I am. I included it because children understand it at once, and grown men need to have it explained.

But look at what it asks for. The story does not say that the son must never be impatient or that he must stop working his field. It asks that the old man sit at the table. That is enough for you to aim at. Your mother cannot be kept from growing old, and you cannot be kept from being tired. She can still be at the table.

What does that mean on a Sunday, at the eleventh question? I think it means attending only to this answer. You are worn down less by the question than by the ten before it and the hundreds you expect. She is not asking eleven questions. Each time, she is asking one. If you can answer that one, even shortly, with your hand on her shoulder, you have done all that was needed. Then she asks again, and you are asked for no more than that.

I must be fair to you here, because I have written a great deal about self-denial and did not always see who did the washing afterwards. A person who preaches simple work should notice who is doing it. If you have brothers or sisters, her place at the table is theirs to lay too. Do not hint. Hints are a kind of pride; they let us suffer and be right. Say it directly: I need you here on Saturdays from ten until four. If there is no brother or sister, ask a neighbour, a parish, a paid nurse, whatever help your town provides. To let someone else do a good deed is also a good deed.

And go outside. I mean this seriously. When my head was bad I went out and mowed with the men, and after an hour I had no head at all, only arms and grass. You may not have a meadow. Scrub a step, dig a pot of earth, walk until you are out of breath. The body has its own way of putting down what the mind insists on carrying.

About the shouting: go and tell her you are sorry. She will not know what for. Tell her anyway, because you will know, and then let it be finished. Remorse that goes on for weeks is of no use to her. It sits down in the chair where your patience ought to be.

If you feel nothing but dread for many days together, or are afraid of what you might do when you are angry, leave her for an hour with someone safe and speak to a doctor about yourself. Nobody should carry that in secret.

It is evening as I finish this. Your mother is probably asking you something. Go and answer her, only this once.
