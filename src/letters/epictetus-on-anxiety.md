Epictetus to a student who cannot stop worrying.

You have a presentation on Thursday, and you have already given it forty times in your head. In every version you forget your words, the room goes quiet, and someone important looks at a watch. You have not slept well since Sunday. You ask me to teach you not to care.

I won't. You should care about your work. But you have attached your care to the wrong end of it.

When I spoke to my students about anxiety, I asked them to think of a musician. Alone in his room he plays well and his hands are loose. Put him in front of a crowd and he sweats. Why? The strings are the same. The song is the same. What changed is that he now wants something besides playing well. He wants them to clap. Their hands belong to them, though, and he has no strings tied to those. So he trembles, and he plays worse for it.

You are that musician. You know your material. What frightens you is the faces.

Ask, then, what you are demanding. Say it out loud, because a demand sounds different in the open air: "I demand that eight people be impressed with me on Thursday." Do you hear it? You might as well demand good weather. You can prepare thoroughly, speak clearly and answer honestly when you don't know. Eight opinions will then form in eight heads you cannot enter. Prepare for the first part as hard as you like. The second part was never your job.

This is simple to understand, and you will have forgotten it by supper. That is how it goes with students. They recite the lesson perfectly in the schoolroom, and then someone important frowns at them and they go pale like anyone else. Knowing is the smaller half. So let me give you some training.

Catch the worry when it arrives and question it like a guard at a gate. Where do you come from? Are you a fact, or a guess wearing a fact's clothes? "The presentation is on Thursday" is a fact. Let it through. "It will be a disaster" has no papers. It may wait outside.

Next, strip the dreaded thing down. Not "humiliation," but: I lose my place, I look at my notes, some seconds go by, I continue. Those are the events. Everything else you painted on afterwards. I don't promise that you will enjoy those seconds. I only say that they are seconds.

Practise on small losses, because a small loss costs little and teaches the same lesson as a large one. I once owned an iron lamp that stood beside my household gods. A thief took it. I told myself that the next day I would buy one made of clay, and I did. Nobody's life was changed. But a man who can stay calm about a lamp has begun to learn to stay calm about a reputation. Start with the spilled cup, the late bus, the message left on read. These are your gymnasium, and the membership is free.

Then, when Thursday comes, carry two intentions into the room. One is to present the work well. The other is to keep your own good sense whatever the faces do. You may fail at the first through bad luck, a broken screen, a hostile question. Nobody in that room can make you fail at the second unless you help them.

I should add a word about your body, since you blame yourself for the pounding heart and the dry mouth. A pale face is no disgrace. Even a wise man may start at a clap of thunder before his reason has caught up. The first jolt is not up to you. What you say to yourself after it is. So let your heart pound, and go on speaking.

If the fear is there every day, with no Thursday to explain it, or if it keeps you from eating, sleeping or leaving the house, see a physician. A person with a fever does not argue with the fever. He gets treatment, and choosing treatment is one of the things that belongs to you.

Now close this letter and go over your notes once. Once is preparation. The forty-first time is only fear, rehearsing itself.
