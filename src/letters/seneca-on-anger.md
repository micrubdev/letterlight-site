Seneca greets his Lucilius.

You tell me you shouted at a colleague this week over something so small you are now ashamed to name it. You say it happens more often than you would like: in traffic, at home, in meetings. Afterwards you are sorry, but in the moment the anger seems entirely justified. You ask whether there is any cure.

There is, though it takes practice, the way all good habits do. I once wrote a whole treatise on this subject for my brother Novatus, who asked me the same question you are asking now. I will not repeat all of it, but I can give you the parts that have helped me most.

Begin by seeing anger clearly. While it lasts, it feels like strength. It feels like honesty, as if at last you were saying what you really think. But look at an angry man from the outside. His face is red, his voice is too loud, his words come out wrong, and he does things he must later apologise for. Anger promises to defend us and then makes us look foolish. It is a kind of madness that lasts a few minutes, and we let it in because, while it lasts, it flatters us.

Notice too how little the cause usually deserves. A colleague is late. A driver cuts in front of you. Someone speaks to you in a tone you dislike. Ask yourself, honestly: was this meant as an insult, or was it carelessness, tiredness, a bad morning? Most of what makes us angry was not aimed at us at all. People are mostly busy with their own troubles. We take personally what was only clumsy.

And when someone does mean to wound you, ask whether it is worth handing them the power to ruin your day. The one who provokes you wants a reaction. Why give him exactly what he wants?

Now to the practical part.

Delay. Nothing helps more. Anger is strongest at the beginning and weakens with time, like a fire with no new wood. When you feel it rising, do not speak, do not write, do not decide anything. Say to yourself, "I will deal with this in an hour." Walk out of the room if you must. Most of the time you will find that, an hour later, the thing has shrunk to its proper size.

Watch your body. Anger shows itself in the flesh before it reaches the tongue: the jaw tightens, the breath grows short, the voice begins to rise. Learn your own signs. When you catch them early, lower your voice on purpose, unclench your hands, slow your breathing. It is hard to stay furious while speaking softly.

Do not let anger punish. There is a story told of Plato, that once, when he was angry with a slave, he stopped with his arm raised and stood there. A friend asked what he was doing. He said he was punishing an angry man, meaning himself. Whatever needs correcting can be corrected later, calmly, when you can judge fairly what it deserves.

Avoid what you know will provoke you, at least while you are tired or hungry. Do not start difficult conversations late at night. Do not read messages that will anger you just before sleep. There is no shame in knowing your weak hours and protecting them.

Keep an account each evening. When the lamp is out and the house is quiet, I go over my day. Where did I lose my temper? Was it worth it? What could I have said instead? I do not judge myself harshly. I only look, the way a careful steward looks at his accounts. You will be surprised how often the answer is simply, "I was tired, and I did not need to say that."

Forgive more easily. You will need forgiveness yourself, many times over. Every one of us has been careless, rude and thoughtless. If you demand perfection from others, you will be angry all your life.

I do not ask you to feel nothing. A good man may be grieved by injustice and act firmly against it. But firmness does not need anger. A judge who sentences calmly is more just than one who sentences in a rage, and more respected too.

If you find that your anger frightens the people around you, or that you cannot stop yourself once it begins, speak to a physician or a counsellor. That is not weakness. It is good sense.

Try the delay this week, and only that. Write and tell me how many times it saved you.
