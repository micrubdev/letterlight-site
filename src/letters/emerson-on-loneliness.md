My dear young friend,

Your letter interested me very much, because you describe a loneliness that few people talk about. It is not that you are alone. You have colleagues, a family, people you see at weekends. It is that you feel alone among them. You laugh at the right moments, you agree when agreement is expected, and you come home feeling as if no one has seen you at all.

I know this feeling well. As a young minister I stood in front of a congregation every Sunday and said what was expected, and I have rarely felt more alone than I did in that pulpit. I gave up the post in the end. It was the loneliest decision of my life, and also the beginning of my real friendships.

Here is what I learned. Much of the loneliness you describe comes from not being yourself in company. You arrive at a dinner and quietly set aside your own opinions, your odd enthusiasms, your sense of humour, and put on a more agreeable person instead. That person is the one they talk to. Of course you feel unseen. You are not there.

I do not tell you to become rude or difficult. I tell you to trust yourself a little more. Say the thing you actually think, kindly but plainly. Mention the strange book you are reading. Admit that you did not enjoy the film everyone else liked. You will be surprised. Some people will lose interest, and that is information. Others will lean in, and say, "I thought so too, but I didn't want to say it." Those people are the beginning of friendship.

It is easy to be yourself when you are alone. It is easy to go along with everyone when you are in a crowd. The hard thing, and the thing worth learning, is to stay yourself in the middle of other people, cheerfully, without making a fuss about it. When you can do that, you will find that loneliness loosens its grip, because you are finally present in your own life.

Spend some time alone on purpose, too. Not the restless alone of an evening spent scrolling, but real solitude: a walk in the woods, an hour with a notebook, a morning with nothing scheduled. Nature is the best teacher I know for this. Stand under a tall tree for a while and you remember that you are part of something very large and very old, and that it does not require you to perform. In solitude you get to know your own mind, and then you have something to bring to other people.

As for friends, do not try to collect many. One or two true friends are worth a hundred acquaintances. A friend, to my mind, is a person with whom you may think aloud, and be sincere. Look around your life for anyone who might be that person. Perhaps someone you have only spoken to at the edges, at work or in the neighbourhood, who once said something that made you think: there is someone real. Invite them for a walk. Friendship asks for a little courage at the start.

And be patient. Every day is a fresh start; yesterday's awkward conversation does not bind you. You may have spent years being agreeable. It will take some months to become yourself again in company. Begin with small honesties and build from there. Tell one person this week something true about yourself that you usually keep back.

You are better company than you think. Let people meet you, and see what they make of the real one.
