To one who is caring for an ageing father,

I have read your letter with the attention I give to any patient's account of his symptoms, and I will answer you as a physician first and a philosopher second.

You tell me your father's health has declined. He can no longer live alone, and so you have taken on his care, along with your work and your own household. You manage his medicines, his meals, his appointments, his nights of confusion. You say you have not slept properly in months, that you have stopped seeing friends, and that you feel guilty whenever you think of anything other than him. You ask whether this is simply what a good child must endure.

Let me tell you plainly: no. What you describe is not virtue carried to its height. It is a good trait pushed into an extreme, and extremes do harm.

I have spent my life arguing that the right way in almost every matter lies in the middle. Generosity sits between meanness and wasting everything one has. Courage sits between cowardice and recklessness. In the same way, devotion to a parent sits between neglect on one side and, on the other, a self-sacrifice so complete that the carer collapses. Neglect is plainly wrong. But the second extreme is also wrong, though it is praised more often. A child who destroys his own health in caring for his father has not fulfilled the duty better. He has only ensured that, soon, there will be two patients instead of one.

I do not say this from a comfortable distance. For many years I served as a physician at the court in Cairo. I would ride there early each morning, and when I returned in the afternoon, faint with hunger, I would find my house full of people waiting to be seen: rich and poor, friends and strangers. I treated them until night, and by then I was often so tired I could barely speak. I know what it is to be needed by everyone and to have nothing left for oneself. I also know that a physician who never rests begins to make mistakes.

So here is my prescription. Follow it as you would follow a physician's orders for your father.

Sleep. A tired mind judges poorly, loses patience and falls ill. If your nights are broken by his needs, find a way to share them: a sibling, a paid helper, a neighbour who will take one night a week. Even one full night of sleep in seven will restore you more than you expect.

Eat regular meals, at a table if you can. Carers often live on scraps eaten standing up. The body is the instrument through which you serve him. Keep it in working order.

Walk every day, even briefly. Moderate exercise clears the mind and steadies the temper. I have advised it to patients for decades, and I have never had cause to regret the advice.

Keep at least one hour a week that belongs to you alone. Read, study, visit a friend, sit in silence. This is not selfishness. It is the rest that lets you return.

Share the work, and accept that others will do it differently. Your brother may not fold the blanket as you would. The day nurse may speak to him less tenderly. Unless something is truly harmful, let it go. Perfect care is not the standard. Steady, decent care over a long time is.

And understand that the law itself sets limits. In my code of law I wrote that if a parent's mind fails, a child should try to care for him as his own wishes would have been. But I also wrote that if the child truly cannot bear it, he may step back and arrange for others to care for his parent properly. The tradition does not ask you to destroy yourself. It asks that your father be cared for well. Sometimes that means by your hands, and sometimes by the hands of others whom you choose and oversee.

If you notice signs in yourself of a deeper illness, such as constant hopelessness, sleep that never restores you, or thoughts that frighten you, consult a physician about your own health. Do not wait until your father is settled. That day may be long in coming.

You are doing a good thing. Do it in a way that you can keep doing.
