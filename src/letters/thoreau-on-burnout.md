Dear friend,

You write that you are exhausted, and not from any single great labour. It is the accumulation that has worn you down: messages, meetings, errands, bills, small promises made to many people. You say you work from waking until sleep and yet never feel that anything is finished. You ask how a man who went to live by a pond would advise someone who cannot leave his desk.

I will not tell you to go and build a cabin in the woods. Most people cannot, and fewer should. But I can tell you what I learned there, and some of it may travel to your desk.

The first lesson was arithmetic. When I lived at Walden I kept careful accounts of every cent I spent: on boards, nails, beans, rice, molasses. I wanted to know what living actually cost. The answer surprised me. It cost much less than my neighbours supposed, and so I needed to work for wages much less than they did. A few weeks of hired labour in a year covered my necessities, and the rest of my time was my own.

I do not expect your accounts to come out the same. But try the experiment. Write down what you truly need to live decently, and compare it with what you are working to pay for. Many of us are tired not because life requires so much, but because we have agreed to a life that does. A larger house, a newer carriage, more clothes than we wear: each one must be paid for in hours, and the hours are taken from our sleep, our friends and our walks.

The second lesson was about attention. Most of what exhausts us is not hard work but scattered work. A hundred small tasks, each interrupting the last, tire the mind more than one long task done with care. When I hoed my beans, I hoed beans, and my thoughts had room to wander and return. Try doing one thing at a time, for an hour, with the door shut. Let the other ninety-nine wait. They will still be there, and most of them will turn out to have mattered less than they claimed.

The third lesson was the outdoors. I have always needed long walks, several hours of them on a good day, through fields and woods with no destination. Plenty of people would call that a waste of time. I counted it the most useful thing I did. A tired mind is not refreshed by more indoor busyness. It needs sky, weather, distance. Even a half-hour walk without your telephone, in whatever green place is near you, will begin to loosen what the desk has tightened.

Now, to the hard part. To recover from burnout, you will have to disappoint some people. You cannot cut away obligations without someone noticing the cut. Choose carefully, explain honestly, and then hold your ground. Most people will adjust more quickly than you fear. The ones who will not adjust may be the ones who were taking the most.

Look especially at the things you do only because others expect them. A committee you joined years ago. A habit of answering every message within the hour. A standard of tidiness that no guest has ever noticed. Ask of each one: if I were starting fresh, would I choose this? If the answer is no, begin to set it down.

Do not try to change everything in a week. That would only be one more project, one more task to fail at. Choose a single thing to remove, and one hour to protect, and begin there. Next week, choose another.

And sleep. At the pond I rose early, with the light, and I recommend the habit, which means going to bed at a sensible hour. A great deal of what feels like despair at night turns out, by morning, to be fatigue.

If your exhaustion goes so deep that rest does not touch it, or you find no pleasure in anything for weeks, see a doctor. That may be an illness of its own, and it deserves treatment.

Write to me after your first long walk, and tell me what you noticed.
