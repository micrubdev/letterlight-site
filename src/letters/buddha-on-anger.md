Dear friend,

You write that you are angry much of the time. At your family, at the news, at people who wronged you years ago and have long since forgotten it. You say the anger feels like fire in your chest, and that you are tired of carrying it, but you do not know how to put it down.

Thank you for writing so honestly. That you are tired of your anger is already the beginning of freedom from it.

Let us look at anger together, calmly, the way one might look at a storm from the shelter of a doorway.

Anger arises when something we do not want comes to us, or when something we want is taken away. Someone speaks harshly. Someone treats us unfairly. Our plans are blocked. The mind says, "This should not be," and the heat begins. This is natural. It does not mean you are a bad person. It means you are a person.

But see what happens next. The mind does not let the moment pass. It replays it. "He said this to me. She did that. They should not have." Each time the story is told again, the fire is fed again. Many people are not angry at what happened. They are angry at the story, which they have repeated a hundred times since.

It is said that a man once came to me and poured out abuse, insult after insult. When he had finished, I asked him: if you prepare food for a guest and the guest does not eat it, to whom does the food belong? He said it would still be his. So it is with abuse, I told him. I do not accept it. It remains with you.

You can do the same. When someone offers you anger, you are not obliged to take it into your own house.

Here are some practices that may help. Try them gently, without forcing.

Notice anger in the body before it becomes words. There is often a tightening in the jaw, heat in the face, a quickening of the heart. When you feel it, stop. Do not push it away and do not obey it. Simply say to yourself, "Anger is here." Naming it puts a little space between you and the feeling.

Then go to the breath. Breathe in slowly, knowing you are breathing in. Breathe out slowly, knowing you are breathing out. Do this ten times. The breath is always with you, wherever you are, and it is a cool place to rest while the heat passes.

Look at the one who angered you, and try to see their suffering. People who act cruelly are usually caught in craving and fear of their own. This does not excuse what they did. But it is hard to stay furious with someone once you see that they, too, are burning.

Practise kindness on purpose, beginning with yourself. Sit quietly and wish yourself well: may I be safe, may I be at ease. Then wish the same for someone you love, then for someone you hardly know, and finally, when you are ready, for the person you are angry with. At first the words may feel empty. Repeat them anyway. A path is made by walking it many times.

Ask what the anger is protecting. Often, beneath anger, there is hurt or fear. The fire is guarding something tender. If you can find what it guards and care for that directly, the fire has less work to do.

Do not hurry. Anger built up over many years will not leave in a week. Each time you pause instead of striking out, you weaken the habit a little. Each time you strike out, you need not despair. Notice, learn, and begin again. That is the whole of the practice.

Notice, too, who suffers most from a grudge. The person you are angry with may be asleep, untroubled, while you lie awake rehearsing what you should have said.

If your anger ever leads you to hurt others or yourself, please seek help from someone trained to give it. Compassion includes accepting help.

Tonight, try ten slow breaths before you sleep, and let that be enough for one day.
