My dear friend,

You write that you have fallen into the habit of measuring yourself against everyone you know. A friend from school has written a book. A cousin has bought a fine house. A colleague is praised in every meeting. Each piece of good news about someone else lands on you like a small stone, and by the end of the week you are carrying a heavy bag of them. You ask how to stop.

I am glad you wrote. This is a common trouble, and I have known it myself.

When I was at Harvard I finished somewhere in the middle of my class, neither brilliant nor dull. My younger brothers, Edward and Charles, were the family's bright hopes, and Edward finished first in his class. For years I watched other young men stride into the professions while I taught school and wondered what I was for. I did not find my own work until I stopped measuring myself by theirs.

Here is what I learned, slowly.

Envy rests on a mistake. When you envy someone, you imagine that if you had their life, you would be happy. But you would not have their life. You would have their circumstances with your own soul inside them, and that is a very different thing. Their book was written out of their experience, their house suits their needs, their praise answers their work. None of it would fit you. You are comparing a coat made for someone else with the one you are wearing, and wondering why theirs looks better.

Each person is given a particular gift. I believe this firmly. There is something you can see, or do, or say, that no one else can in quite the same way. It may be small. It may not be valued by the market or praised in meetings. But it is yours, and the world is poorer if you neglect it while staring at your neighbour's. Your task is not to become them. Your task is to become more fully yourself.

Notice where your attention goes. Every hour spent studying another person's success is an hour not spent on your own work. Envy is very expensive in this way. It takes your energy and gives you nothing back. If you must look at others, look to learn, not to measure.

So here is what I would suggest.

Spend time alone each day, away from news of other people. A walk, an hour with a notebook, a quiet chair by the window. In solitude the voices of comparison grow quiet, and you can hear your own thoughts again. I have found the woods more useful for this than any library.

When envy stings, ask what it is pointing at. Sometimes it points to something you truly want and have not dared to pursue. Then the envy is useful. Take it as a signal and begin, in a small way, to move toward that thing. Other times it points to something you do not really want, only want to be admired for. Then let it go.

Do your own work without waiting for applause. Much of what I wrote was misunderstood at first, and some of it was attacked. If I had needed the approval of others to keep going, I would have stopped. The work itself must be its own reward. Then the successes of others cannot rob you, because you were never competing for the same prize.

Be generous with praise. It is a strange thing, but when you sincerely congratulate someone, the envy loosens. Try it this week. Write to the friend who published the book and tell them what you liked about it. You may find that their joy becomes a little of yours.

Remember that you see only the outside of other lives. Behind the fine house there may be debts. Behind the praise there may be fear. Every person you envy is carrying something you cannot see.

You are not behind. You are on a different road, and it was never a race.

Write to me after a week of this, and tell me what you noticed.
