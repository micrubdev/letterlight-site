Dear friend,

You write that your father died in the spring, and that now, months later, everyone around you seems to expect you to be finished with it. You go to work, you answer emails, you smile at the right moments. But at night you lie awake and the grief comes back as if it had only been waiting for the lights to go out.

I am an old man and I have buried many people I loved: my mother before I could remember her face, my father, my brothers, and five of my own children. I will not pretend that I know exactly what you feel. No one knows another person's grief from the inside. But I can tell you some things I have learned, slowly and against my will.

The first is that grief does not keep a timetable. The world has its calendars, but the heart has none. Some losses we carry lightly after a year. Others we carry for the rest of our lives, and that is not a sickness. It is simply the measure of what that person was to us. If you are still grieving, it is because there is still love with nowhere to go. Do not be ashamed of it.

The second thing is harder to say. When someone dies, we often grieve not only for them but for what we did not do. The visit we postponed. The quarrel we never mended. The words we assumed there would be time to say. I have lain awake over such things myself, and I know how they gnaw. But listen. Your father did not love you for the visits you made. He loved you because you were his. The love was given before you could earn it and it does not need to be repaid in full. Forgive yourself as he would have forgiven you, and probably long ago already did.

The third thing I learned only late. For many years I was terrified of death, my own and other people's. I tried to understand it the way one studies a problem, and the more I thought, the more frightened I became. What finally gave me some peace was not an argument. It was noticing what remained when someone died. Their money and their possessions were divided up. Their plans came to nothing. But the love they had given was still there, at work in the people they had loved. It had passed into us. It went on.

This is what I would ask you to do with your grief: let it become love that goes somewhere. Not all at once, and not as a duty. But your father's patience, his way of fixing things, the way he spoke to strangers: these do not have to end with him. You can carry them on. Every time you do something kind in the way that he would have done it, he is a little less gone.

Meanwhile, be gentle with yourself. Work with your hands if you can. I have always found that digging, chopping wood, walking a long way, calms a mind that thought alone only stirs up. Speak about him to people who knew him. Let yourself be looked after. And if the nights become too long and too dark, if you begin to feel that you cannot go on, tell someone at once. A friend, a doctor, a stranger at the end of a telephone line. Think of it as the same love, turned toward yourself.

We do not live for ourselves alone, my friend. We live by love, and your father's love has not left you. It has only changed its address.

With affection,
L. T.
