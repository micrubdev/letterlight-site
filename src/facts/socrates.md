date: 2026-11-25
facts:
  - title: "He stood still thinking for a whole day and night"
    text: "One summer on campaign, Plato says, Socrates began working on a problem at dawn and stayed where he was. That evening some Ionians brought out their mats and slept in the open to see whether he would stand all night. He did. At sunrise he said a prayer and walked off."
    source: "Plato, Symposium, translated by Benjamin Jowett"
    source_url: "https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Symposium_(Jowett)"
    evidence: "slept in the open air that they might watch him and see whether he would stand all night"
  - title: "He marched barefoot over ice"
    text: "In a severe frost on the same campaign, the other soldiers who went outdoors wrapped their feet in felt and fleeces. Socrates went barefoot in his ordinary clothes and marched better than they did. They glared at him, because he seemed to despise them."
    source: "Plato, Symposium, translated by Benjamin Jowett"
    source_url: "https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Symposium_(Jowett)"
    evidence: "Socrates with his bare feet on the ice and in his ordinary dress marched better than the other soldiers who had shoes"
  - title: "An oracle said no one was wiser"
    text: "His friend Chaerephon went to Delphi and asked whether anyone was wiser than Socrates. The answer was no. In Plato's account of the trial, Socrates says the answer puzzled him, because he didn't think himself wise."
    source: "Plato, Apology, translated by Benjamin Jowett"
    source_url: https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html
    evidence: "the Pythian prophetess answered that there was no man wiser"
  - title: "He refused an order to make an arrest"
    text: "When the Thirty ruled Athens, they ordered Socrates and four others to fetch a man called Leon to be executed. The other four went. Socrates went home. He said he might have died for it if the government had not fallen soon afterwards."
    source: "Plato, Apology, translated by Benjamin Jowett"
    source_url: https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html
    evidence: "bade us bring Leon the Salaminian from Salamis, as they wanted to execute him"
  - title: "He asked for free dinners as his punishment"
    text: "Found guilty, he was allowed to suggest his own penalty. He proposed meals at public expense, an honour he thought he deserved more than the winner of an Olympic chariot race. Then, with friends offering to pay, he suggested a fine."
    source: "Plato, Apology, translated by Benjamin Jowett"
    source_url: https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html
    evidence: "a reward which he deserves far more than the citizen who has won the prize at Olympia in the horse or chariot race"
  - title: "He took up poetry in prison"
    text: "Waiting for his execution, he obeyed a recurring dream and composed a few verses. He didn't think he could invent stories, so he took some fables of Aesop that he knew and turned them into verse."
    source: "Plato, Phaedo, translated by Benjamin Jowett"
    source_url: https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/phaedo.html
    evidence: "which I had ready at hand and knew, and turned them into verse"
  - title: "He left no writings"
    text: "Socrates wrote nothing that has come down to us. What we know of his conversations comes from other people, above all Plato and Xenophon."
    source: "Encyclopædia Britannica (1911), Socrates"
    source_url: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Socrates
    evidence: "Socrates left no writings behind him"
  - title: "Did he carve statues on the way to the Acropolis?"
    status: legend
    text: "His father was a sculptor. More than 500 years after his death, visitors were still shown a group of the Graces near the Acropolis that was supposed to be his work. We have only the supposition."
    source: "Encyclopædia Britannica (1911), Socrates"
    source_url: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Socrates
    evidence: "a group of the Graces, supposed to be his work, was still to be seen on the road to the Acropolis"
---
Socrates is one of the best-known people in history, and everything we know about him comes from someone else. Most of these stories are told by Plato, his student, who was also a great dramatist.
