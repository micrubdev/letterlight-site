description: "True stories and facts about Seneca: the noisy flat over a bathhouse, his icy New Year plunge, a year without meat, and one famous forgery, all with sources."
date: 2026-11-25
facts:
  - title: "He lodged above a public bath"
    text: "He complained that he lived right over a bathing establishment. Through the floor came grunting weightlifters, splashing swimmers, a hair-plucker advertising in a shrill voice, and sausage sellers shouting their wares. He was trying to study."
    source: "Seneca, Letter 56, translated by Richard M. Gummere"
    source_url: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_56
    evidence: "I have lodgings right over a bathing establishment."
  - title: "He started the year with a cold plunge"
    text: "Seneca was a self-confessed cold-water enthusiast who used to greet the first of January by jumping into the water of the Virgo aqueduct. In old age he admitted that his cold bath was now just short of hot."
    source: "Seneca, Letter 83, translated by Richard M. Gummere"
    source_url: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_83
    evidence: "used to inaugurate the first of the year with a plunge into the Virgo aqueduct"
  - title: "He got so seasick he jumped off the boat"
    text: "On a rough crossing he felt too ill to fear drowning. He ordered the pilot to make for the shore, and before they had anchored he let himself down into the sea in his cloak, then scrambled over the rocks."
    source: "Seneca, Letter 53, translated by Richard M. Gummere"
    source_url: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_53
    evidence: "clad as I was in my cloak, let myself down into the sea, just as a cold-water bather should"
  - title: "He gave up meat for a year"
    text: "As a young man he was won over by a teacher's case against eating animals, and by the end of the year he found it easy and pleasant. Then avoiding certain meats came to look like a sign of a foreign cult, and he stopped because his father asked him to."
    source: "Seneca, Letter 108, translated by Richard M. Gummere"
    source_url: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_108
    evidence: "at the end of a year the habit was as pleasant as it was easy"
  - title: "He kept a very hard pillow into old age"
    text: "His teacher Attalus recommended a pillow that did not give way. Decades later Seneca still used one so hard that his body left no mark on it. He also gave up oysters and mushrooms for good."
    source: "Seneca, Letter 108, translated by Richard M. Gummere"
    source_url: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_108
    evidence: "now, old as I am, I use one so hard that it leaves no trace after pressure"
  - title: "His own house told him he was old"
    text: "Visiting his country house, he grumbled that the building was crumbling and the plane trees were neglected. The bailiff answered that the house was simply old. Seneca had seen that house built, and the visit made him face his own age."
    source: "Seneca, Letter 12, translated by Richard M. Gummere"
    source_url: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_12
    evidence: "And this was the house which grew under my own hands!"
  - title: "He spent eight years in exile"
    text: "He was banished to the island of Corsica in the year 41, under the emperor Claudius. He filled the time with study and writing, and with some flattery that he hoped would bring him home."
    source: "Encyclopædia Britannica (1911), Seneca"
    source_url: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Seneca
    evidence: "There eight weary years of waiting were relieved by study and authorship"
  - title: "Did he write letters to Saint Paul?"
    status: legend
    text: "Letters between Seneca and Saint Paul were known as early as Saint Jerome's day. They are forgeries, made because some early Christians admired his ethics enough to count him as one of their own."
    source: "Encyclopædia Britannica (1911), Seneca"
    source_url: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Seneca
    evidence: "led to the forgery of a correspondence between St Paul and Seneca which was known to Jerome"

Seneca told a surprising number of stories against himself. Most of these come straight from the letters he wrote near the end of his life.
